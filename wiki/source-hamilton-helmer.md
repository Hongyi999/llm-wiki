# Business strategy with Hamilton Helmer (author of 7 Powers)

**Guest**: Hamilton Helmer  
**Published**: 2024-05-05  
**Duration**: 1:08:08  
**YouTube**: [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hKq1_KPSqy0](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hKq1_KPSqy0)

## Executive summary

Hamilton Helmer, author of the seminal strategy book 7 Powers, explains how sustainable competitive advantage is built through structures that provide both a benefit (cost or price advantage) and a barrier (durability against imitation). He reveals that his thinking has evolved: strategy should be considered from day one, not just post-product-market-fit, because early decisions about business propositions can tilt probabilities toward power. For startups, he narrows the actionable powers to four — counter-positioning, scale economies, switching costs, and network economies — and explains their natural sequencing. Helmer debunks common misconceptions about data as a moat, flywheels as network economies, and operational excellence as power, while drawing a sharp distinction between network effects (common) and network economies (rare and material).

## Key ideas

1. **Power = Benefit + Barrier**: Sustainable competitive advantage requires both a benefit (lower cost or higher price than competitors) and a barrier (something durable that prevents imitation). Warren Buffett's 'castle protected by a moat' captures this — but you must verify it's a castle, not a shack.

2. **Four powers for startups**: Of the seven powers, only four are actionable for early-stage companies: counter-positioning (your starting refuge from incumbents), then scale economies, switching costs, and network economies. Branding, process power, and resource power can be set aside initially.

3. **Network effects vs. network economies**: Many companies have network effects, but few have network economies — the distinction is materiality. Uber and Lyft have network effects but not network economies because the advantage isn't large enough to materially tilt margins. The 'flywheel' pitch is almost always overstated.

4. **Operational excellence is not power**: Echoing Michael Porter, Helmer argues that execution excellence is essential but almost always imitable. It's a treadmill you must run on to survive, but it's not a barrier. Process power (execution so complex it becomes opaque and inimitable) exists but is exceptionally rare — think Toyota or TSMC.

5. **AI as electricity, not as a new power**: Helmer sees AI's biggest impact in the 'tertiary' category — existing businesses made better by the technology, analogous to how electricity transformed factory floors. He does not see an eighth power emerging from AI, but notes AI could create powerful scale economies, switching costs, or network economies for specific businesses.

## Concepts covered

- **[[seven-powers|7 Powers Framework]]** — A comprehensive framework identifying seven sources of sustainable competitive advantage: counter-positioning, scale economies, switching costs, network economies, branding, process power, and cornered resource.
- **[[seven-powers|Power Progression]]** — The insight that different types of power become available at different stages of a business lifecycle, with counter-positioning first and branding/process power last.
- **[[seven-powers|Benefit and Barrier Test]]** — The 'to be or not to be' test for power: does the company have a cost or price advantage (benefit) that competitors cannot replicate (barrier)?
- **[[seven-powers|Network Economies vs. Network Effects]]** — Network effects are common but network economies — where the value benefit is material enough to drive significant margin differences — are rare.
- **[[counter-positioning|Counter-Positioning]]** — A startup's initial refuge from incumbents where the new business model or approach is one the incumbent cannot adopt without damaging their existing business.
