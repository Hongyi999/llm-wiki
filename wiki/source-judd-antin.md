# Source: The UX Research Reckoning — Macro/Middle/Micro Framework ft. Judd Antin

**Guest**: Judd Antin (Former VP UX Research & Design Studio, Airbnb; Former Research Leader, Meta)
**Published**: 2024-01-04
**Duration**: ~1h 15m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L6RKi9ZvkT4

## Executive summary

Judd Antin argues that the UX research discipline of the last 15 years is dying and must evolve. The core problem is that too much research lives in a "middle range" — interesting questions about user attitudes and behaviors that are neither strategic enough (macro) nor precise enough (micro) to drive business impact. Research teams were hired en masse during the ZIRP era but positioned as a reactive service function, producing insights that were fascinating but difficult to operationalize, ultimately leading to disproportionate layoffs when budgets tightened.

## Key ideas

1. **Macro / Middle / Micro research framework** — Macro research is strategic, forward-looking, business-focused: competitive landscape analysis, concept cars, TAM studies, inputs to annual planning. Micro research is laser-focused and fast: usability testing, button copy optimization, AB test interpretation. Middle range is the blobby zone in between — e.g., "How do Airbnb hosts feel about payment options?" — yielding insights that are interesting but not pointed enough to drive action. The thesis: too much research lives in the middle. Companies should shift effort toward macro (for strategic planning) and micro (for rapid product improvement).

2. **User-centered performance** — A term Antin coined for customer-obsession practices that are symbolic rather than focused on learning. Examples: a PM asking a researcher to "validate our assumptions" at the end of a product process (too late to matter); executive listening sessions where leaders want to feel close to customers but aren't seeking to change anything; focus groups as checkbox exercises. The antidote: "We don't validate, we falsify. We are looking to be wrong."

3. **The multimillion-dollar button** — At Airbnb, micro-level research revealed that users were dropping off the purchase funnel because a button's call-to-action text felt like it would initiate a purchase when it was actually just the next step. Changing seven characters made Airbnb millions of dollars by adding approximately 1% to conversion. This happened in roughly 48 hours — demolishing the trope that "research slows us down."

4. **The five tools of a great researcher** — (1) Formative/generative research (ethnographic, forward-looking), (2) Evaluative research (usability testing), (3) Rigorous survey design, (4) Applied statistics (essential for interacting with AB test results), (5) Technical skills — historically SQL, now possibly prompt engineering. Researchers should be multi-method Swiss army knives; if all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a qualitative nail.

5. **The vicious cycle of research as service** — Companies hire researchers with good intentions but position them as a reactive service function. Researchers aren't in the room for key decisions, so they end up doing middle-range work that's less impactful. Executives conclude research isn't driving value and cut headcount. The short-circuit: integrate researchers into the product process from beginning to end with consistent relationships. "My metric for success is when they won't have that meeting without you."

6. **Researchers must speak the language of business** — Read the quarterly report, listen to shareholder calls, understand the conversion funnel, know the OKRs. When a PM asks a middle-range question, a business-literate researcher can reframe it as a pointed investigation of a specific spot in the funnel. Antin created an integrated insights function at Airbnb that unified UX research, market research, NPS data, customer service feedback, and data science into one machine.

7. **Five tropes PMs need to stop believing** — (1) "Research slows us down" — good research can deliver in 48 hours. (2) "I can do my own research" — talking to one user is not research; garbage in, garbage out. (3) "AB test everything" — AB tests tell you what changed, not why; researchers answer the why. (4) The apocryphal Henry Ford quote — "A researcher who asks customers what they want is a bad researcher." (5) "We already knew that" — hindsight bias and narrative fallacy make us think we knew things we didn't.

8. **NPS is broken; use CSAT instead** — NPS uses a flawed 11-point scale, an unlabeled midpoint, a question that doesn't map to real behavior ("How likely are you to recommend Windows 11?"), and produces idiosyncratic results that can't be meaningfully benchmarked across companies. Airbnb's survey science team confirmed that simple customer satisfaction (CSAT) questions are more precise, more correlated to business outcomes, and better in every measurable way.

## Concepts that deserve their own wiki page

- **[[User-centered performance]]** — Customer-obsession practices that are symbolic rather than focused on genuine learning; the organizational theater of caring about users without being willing to be wrong
- **[[Macro-middle-micro research]]** — Framework for categorizing research by altitude: strategic/macro, blobular/middle, and precision/micro, with the argument that middle-range dominance destroys research impact
- **[[Research as service vs. research as partner]]** — The distinction between reactive, siloed research (researchers called in at the end to validate) and integrated research (researchers embedded in the product process from beginning to end)

## Notable quote

> "User-centered performance refers to customer obsession or user-centered practice that is symbolic rather than focused on learning. Every time a PM comes to a researcher at the end of a product process and says, 'Can you just run a quick user study just to validate our assumptions,' that's user-centered performance. It's too late to matter. We got to ship it. What they want is to check the box."

## B10 angle

Antin's "we don't validate, we falsify" mantra connects directly to [[mike-maples-jr|Mike Maples Jr.]]'s emphasis on [[earned secrets]] and being surprised — both argue that the most valuable insights come from seeking to be wrong rather than confirming what you already believe. His macro/middle/micro framework gives researchers and PMs a concrete vocabulary for the problem [[mihika-kapoor|Mihika Kapoor]] solves intuitively at Figma: she skips middle-range research in favor of rapid prototyping (micro) combined with long-range vision (macro). The "multimillion-dollar button" story is also a powerful example of [[jessica-livingston|Jessica Livingston]]'s emphasis on domain expertise — the researchers who found that insight did so because they understood the specific funnel mechanics intimately, not because they ran a generic study.
