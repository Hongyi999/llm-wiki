# Why not asking for what you want is holding you back | Kenneth Berger

**Guest**: Kenneth Berger
**Published**: 2024-05-19
**Duration**: 1:13:36
**YouTube**: [Lenny's Podcast — Kenneth Berger](https://www.lennyspodcast.com/)

## Executive summary

Kenneth Berger — executive coach to startup founders on preventing burnout, formerly the first product manager at Slack and a 10+ year operator/founder — argues that almost every stuck point in a leader's career (burnout, interpersonal conflict, lack of fulfillment) traces back to not asking clearly for what you want. He splits askers into two archetypes (people-pleasers who never ask, control freaks who order) and offers a three-step loop: articulate, ask intentionally, accept the response. He reframes complaints as dreams-in-disguise, insists "anything less than a hell yes is a no," and treats asking-for-what-you-want as an integrity practice, not a negotiation tactic. Personal stories (fired from Slack three times) accompany tactical scripts for feedback and disagree-and-commit.

## Key ideas

1. **Ask for what you want (three steps)**: Articulate → ask intentionally → accept the response, then iterate. Most resistance lives in the gap between knowing and saying out loud.
2. **Dream behind the complaint**: Every complaint implies a dream. People are bad at dreaming but good at complaining — reverse-engineer the complaint to surface the real desired future.
3. **Hell yes or it's a no**: Treat any response short of enthusiastic ("whole-body") yes as a no. Lukewarm "maybes" from your CTO about a May 1 date predict missed milestones.
4. **Two archetypes: people-pleaser vs. control freak**: People-pleasers hope others read their minds; control freaks bark orders. Both avoid the real work of vulnerable, humble, clear asking.
5. **Humble-but-clear asking beats data-armor**: Especially for ICs and PMs, stating "this is what I want, I know I can't make you do it" is more influential than hiding behind A/B results. Your opinion and relationship are legitimate data.
