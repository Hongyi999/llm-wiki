# Improve strategy, influence, and decision-making by understanding your brain | Evan LaPointe

**Guest**: Evan LaPointe  
**Published**: 2024-08-11  
**Duration**: 2:14:39  
**YouTube**: [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GECtyEj9UPA](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GECtyEj9UPA)

## Executive summary

Evan LaPointe, founder of CORE Sciences and four-time founder (including Satellite, acquired by Adobe), teaches teams how neuroscience translates into better products, decisions, and collaboration. He introduces the brain's three systems (safety, reward, purpose), three focus modes (alpha/daydreaming, beta/productivity, gamma/deep thinking), and personality dimensions from the Big Five model to explain why teams get stuck. The conversation covers why most meetings fail (skipping priming), why vision feels 'inconceivable' to low-openness personalities, how to pick an influence character aligned to your personality, why appeal matters more than ability in relationships, and why logical deduction beats inspiration for building company culture. His core thesis is captured by the Dan Pink quote: 'There's a mismatch between what science knows and what business does'—and closing that gap is the highest-leverage thing any team can do.

## Key ideas

1. **The brain's three systems—safety, reward, purpose**: When people feel unsafe in meetings, their objective shifts from contributing to self-protection. The reward system is transactional ('that's not my job'). The purpose system—activated when people understand the impact of their work on others—unlocks the full brain and generates the best outcomes.

2. **Meetings need priming before decision-making**: Most meetings skip priming (aligning on context, principles, and mindset) and jump straight to decision-making, leading to misaligned debates. When one person optimizes for speed and another for accuracy, conflict is inevitable until they surface the misaligned principles underneath.

3. **Personality shapes strategy tolerance**: Big Five openness determines your tolerance for abstract, creative, vision-type thinking. Low openness wires abstract thinking to the brain's pain systems. High conscientiousness can further resist open-ended exploration. Self-awareness and vulnerability about these traits is the fastest way to unblock a team.

4. **Choose your influence character**: Like selecting a video game character, intentionally pick an influence style aligned to your personality—compassion-based caregiver, logic-based devil's advocate, creativity-based explorer—and seek permission to consistently operate that way. Pair this with the right speed: slow (let them fail), moderate (Challenger Sale teaching), or fast (cognitive dissonance).

5. **Relationships = Ability + Trust + Appeal (in reverse order of importance)**: Appeal (what kind of experience are you?) matters most biologically—a brilliant but miserable engineer gets sequestered from information flows. Trust (risk assessment) matters second. Ability matters last. Fix your appeal first before optimizing anything else.

## Concepts covered

- **Brain's Three Systems** — Safety (threat response), reward (transactional motivation), and purpose (impact-driven motivation) systems that shape how people think and act at work.
- **Meeting Priming** — The practice of aligning on context, principles, and mindset before diving into decision-making in meetings.
- **Big Five Personality Model** — A personality framework (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, need for stability) used to understand thinking preferences and team dynamics.
- **Alpha-Beta-Gamma Focus** — Three brain wave states: alpha (daydreaming/shower ideas), beta (productivity/get-stuff-done), gamma (deep analytical thinking)—most teams over-index on beta.
- **Habitat (Company Culture)** — The environment and belief systems of a company that predispose teams to high or low functioning—best built through logical deduction rather than inspirational mission statements.
- **Ability-Trust-Appeal Framework** — A relationship model where appeal (experience of working with someone) matters most, trust (risk assessment) second, and ability (skill/knowledge) third.
