# Source: What Sets Great Teams Apart ft. Lane Shackleton

**Guest**: Lane Shackleton (CPO, Coda; former Group PM, YouTube)
**Published**: 2023-10-01
**Duration**: ~1h 30m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XmgetFMgQZ0

## Executive summary

Lane Shackleton, who has spent 8+ years building Coda's product team, argues that the core job of a product person is to turn ambiguity into clarity. He shares a set of first-principles-derived principles (systems not goals, cathedrals not bricks, learn by making) and team rituals (flash tags, Catalyst, Dory, two-way writeups) that he has collected from studying great teams across industries — from Jerry Seinfeld's comedy system to Pixar's braintrust.

## Key ideas

1. **Systems not goals** — Inspired by Jerry Seinfeld throwing away all his material and rebuilding through a daily system (write one hour every morning, perform every night), Shackleton argues product teams should obsess over default-on systems rather than one-time goals. A team with a system of talking to customers multiple times per week builds better instincts than a team with an OKR to "talk to 10 customers this quarter." At early Coda, Fridays had a standing slot for a customer visit — the team had to have something to show, creating a forcing function for prototyping.

2. **Flash tags** — Originated by Dharmesh Shah at HubSpot and adopted at Coda. A four-level scale for calibrating feedback intensity: FYI (no hill in sight), Suggestion (there's a hill, won't die on it), Recommendation (climbing the hill, thought about this a lot), and Plea (rarely used — this is a hill worth dying on). Used in feedback tables, docs, and even live meetings ("Are you making a recommendation or a plea?"). Critical for leaders whose casual observations get over-interpreted by teams.

3. **Catalyst: the review ritual** — Traditional review forums suffer from two problems: standing attendees (wrong people in the room) and single-threading (one topic at a time, creating bottlenecks). Catalyst solves both: three one-hour blocks per week where the whole company is free, each topic has four roles (Driver, Maker, Braintrust, Interested), multiple topics run in parallel when attendees don't overlap, and the calendar hold gets replaced with specific topic invites the day before. Result: dramatically higher throughput with the right people every time.

4. **Cathedrals not bricks** — The parable of three brick-layers: one moves bricks, one applies cement, one says "we're building a cathedral." Shishir Mehrotra asked Shackleton this pointed question when he was complaining about team performance at YouTube. The insight: show every team member their facet of the cathedral. A single writeup is insufficient — pair it with metrics, directional mocks, a billboard concept, a homepage redesign. Remove the mystery from the vision so people can connect their daily work to the broader arc.

5. **Two-way writeups** — Shackleton traces the evolution from PowerPoint (1980s) to one-way writeups (2000s, Bezos's six-pager memo) to two-way writeups. One-way writeups suffer from: you cannot tell who has read them (watching for the SVP's avatar to appear), discussion gets crammed into 100-pixel comment margins, and mega-threads form on the doc title. Two-way writeups add: a "done reading" button, a Dory table where questions are upvoted by importance, and a sentiment table where everyone rates the proposal (exposing dissent that quiet team members would never voice in meetings).

6. **Seek discomfort: the "oh shit" career metric** — From Seth Godin: moments where you feel "I shouldn't be here" or "I'm underqualified for this" are the moments that stretch you and give you a new foundation. A sharper career growth question than "Do you feel like you're growing?" is: "How many oh-shit moments have you had in the last six months, and what are they?" If the answer is "it's been a long time," you may be stagnating.

7. **The 10% planning rule** — For any execution period, planning should not consume more than 10% of the total time. Planning for a quarter? Spend roughly one week. This is a hard-fought rule born from the mistake of letting planning expand to fill available time, at the expense of actually building and learning.

## B10 angle

Shackleton's flash tags are directly referenced by [[tom-conrad]] as a tool he tries to use (though he notes it does not fully solve the CEO gravity problem — people still over-index on any CEO input). The Catalyst review ritual parallels [[katie-dill]]'s Walk the Store and Product Quality Reviews at Stripe — both are cadence-based cross-functional reviews with structured scoring. Shackleton's "systems not goals" principle is the operational counterpart to [[eric-ries]]'s build-measure-learn loop: both argue that the process of continuous learning matters more than hitting any single target.
