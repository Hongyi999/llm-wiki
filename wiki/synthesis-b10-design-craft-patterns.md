# Synthesis: B10 — Design, craft & founder-led 0→1 convergent patterns

7 patterns that emerge across 21 unique B10 sources. Each is independently articulated by 3+ voices and represents genuine convergence.

## Pattern 1: Quality is not the opposite of speed — it IS the growth lever

The deepest convergence in B10. Dill's 10.5% revenue lift from checkout improvements, Singleton's error-message documentation moat, Butterfield's utility curves (value follows S-curves with quality thresholds), Zlokazov's "99% done ≈ 0%", and Baxley's moral obligation thesis all converge on the same point: quality drives adoption, retention, and revenue. The false dichotomy of "ship fast vs. ship well" is rejected by every B10 design voice. **Implication**: companies that treat craft as a cost center are systematically underinvesting in their most reliable growth lever.

**Voices**: [[source-katie-dill|Dill]], [[source-david-singleton|Singleton]], [[source-stewart-butterfield|Butterfield]], [[source-dmitry-zlokazov|Zlokazov]], [[source-bob-baxley|Baxley]]

## Pattern 2: Founder/leader detail review scales quality

Zlokazov (founders review 100% of shipped screens weekly), Dill (PQRs with CTO and VP Design), Singleton (CTO friction-logs onboarding monthly, does engineer-cations), and Butterfield (divine discontent — calling your product "a giant piece of shit") all describe the same mechanism: when senior leaders personally inspect product quality at the detail level, the entire org calibrates upward. This is not micromanagement — it's quality-standard setting through demonstrated behavior.

**Voices**: [[source-dmitry-zlokazov|Zlokazov]], [[source-katie-dill|Dill]], [[source-david-singleton|Singleton]], [[source-stewart-butterfield|Butterfield]]

## Pattern 3: Small teams create breakthroughs; scale after clarity

Baxley (20 on the Mac, 24 on the iPhone; The Beatles with 4, not 24), Henrickson (4 people built Rippling Time & Attendance), Kapoor (hackathon team of volunteers as 0→1 nucleus), and Conrad (Berkeley Systems constraint — stay in your lane with small teams). The convergence: breakthrough products require a small group with shared vision. Scale people in after the concept is proven, not before.

**Voices**: [[source-bob-baxley|Baxley]], [[source-jeremy-henrickson|Henrickson]], [[source-mihika-kapoor|Kapoor]], [[source-tom-conrad|Conrad]]

## Pattern 4: Dogfooding and user co-creation are non-negotiable

Singleton (co-create with early users via shared Slack channels), Prohovnik (every PM must run a podcast), Gotthilf (growth team's badge failure was a dogfooding failure), Zlokazov (Revolut employees are paid in the product), Kapoor (multi-month staging/dogfooding at Figma), and Monga (build with writers via Product Lab). Six voices independently describe the same pattern: teams that don't use their own product build for abstractions, not humans.

**Voices**: [[source-david-singleton|Singleton]], [[source-maya-prohovnik|Prohovnik]], [[source-gina-gotthilf|Gotthilf]], [[source-dmitry-zlokazov|Zlokazov]], [[source-mihika-kapoor|Kapoor]], [[source-sachin-monga|Monga]]

## Pattern 5: Bet on founders/people, not ideas

Livingston (funded Airbnb despite hating the idea — bet on the cereal box hustlers), Maples (founder-future fit over generic founder quality; 80% of biggest returns came from pivots), Ries (pivot = change strategy, keep vision), Zlokazov (raw intellect + hunger > experience), and Conrad (failure as career accelerant — Pets.com opened doors). The convergence: the idea is the least durable element; the people and their adaptability determine outcomes.

**Voices**: [[source-jessica-livingston|Livingston]], [[source-mike-maples-jr|Maples]], [[source-eric-ries|Ries]], [[source-dmitry-zlokazov|Zlokazov]], [[source-tom-conrad|Conrad]]

## Pattern 6: Systems and rituals outperform goals and processes

Shackleton (systems not goals — daily habits beat quarterly OKRs), Knapp & Zeratsky (change defaults, not willpower — Highlight principle), Dill (Walk the Store as a recurring ritual, not a one-time audit), Shackleton's flash tags + Catalyst reviews, Prohovnik (Eisenhower Matrix as daily practice), and Gotthilf (A/B testing as organic growth system, not a project). The convergence: durable quality comes from default-on systems, not heroic one-time efforts.

**Voices**: [[source-lane-shackleton|Shackleton]], [[source-jake-knapp-john-zeratsky|Knapp & Zeratsky]], [[source-katie-dill|Dill]], [[source-maya-prohovnik|Prohovnik]], [[source-gina-gotthilf|Gotthilf]]

## Pattern 7: The single-player → multiplayer transition is the growth chasm

Ionita (Evernote's failure to bridge personal → team), Monga (Substack's tool-to-network evolution via Recommendations), Gotthilf (Duolingo's mission-driven community as network effect), and Kramer (PLG is really product + marketing, requiring the multiplayer handoff). The convergence: products that stay single-player plateau; the most consequential product decision is how and when to introduce collaborative/network dynamics.

**Voices**: [[source-naomi-ionita|Ionita]], [[source-sachin-monga|Monga]], [[source-gina-gotthilf|Gotthilf]], [[source-emily-kramer|Kramer]]

## Meta-pattern: The craft maturity arc

B10 collectively describes a maturity arc for product craft:

| Stage | Focus | Key B10 voice |
|---|---|---|
| Vision | Inflections + insights + founder-future fit | Maples, Livingston |
| 0→1 | Keeper of the flame; hype as fuel; hackathon nucleation | Kapoor, Ries |
| First users | Co-create; dogfood; kill your darlings | Singleton, Prohovnik, Monga |
| Quality scaling | Design tenets; Walk the Store; founder review | Baxley, Dill, Zlokazov |
| Growth compounding | Mission-led organic; brand voice; tool → network | Gotthilf, Kramer, Ionita |
| Sustainability | Systems over goals; manage energy; career resilience | Shackleton, Knapp & Zeratsky, Johns |

This arc complements B9's GTM maturity arc: B9 describes how to sell what you build, B10 describes how to build what's worth selling.
