# Source: Moving Fast and Navigating Uncertainty ft. Jeremy Henrickson

**Guest**: Jeremy Henrickson (SVP Product, Rippling; formerly CPO, Coinbase)
**Published**: 2023-06-04
**Duration**: ~1h 9m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CsruQYKISYI

## Executive summary

Jeremy Henrickson lays out the Rippling playbook for maintaining velocity at scale: small teams with clear missions, a shared platform that reduces decision complexity, and product leaders who go all the way to ground on their domains. He argues against MVPs in favor of designing for the most complex use case first, and describes the compound startup model where many products built on a single system of record create compounding differentiation.

## Key ideas

1. **The compound startup model** — Rippling builds many businesses (payroll, benefits, IT, device management, time & attendance) on a single platform with one employee system of record. This shared database means every downstream product always has correct data (e.g., "who is this person's manager?"), enabling capabilities that are literally impossible for point solutions to replicate. The activation energy is enormous, but once built, each new product benefits from the platform.

2. **Design for the most complex use case first** — Jeremy is skeptical of MVPs in Rippling's context. When you only design for simple cases, you make architectural assumptions that are extremely difficult to unwind later. Instead, Rippling studies the hardest use cases (e.g., a 10,000-person global company with mission-critical requirements) and builds the model to accommodate them — even if v1 doesn't support them yet. Their global payroll system was designed around six very different countries simultaneously, so adding new countries became a configuration task rather than a re-architecture.

3. **Go and see** — One of Rippling's leadership principles. Leaders must go all the way to ground — talk to the engineer writing the code, study the tax laws of each country, look at the actual data. Jeremy estimates this should consume roughly half of a product leader's time. "What's the point of writing a document if you don't know what you're talking about?"

4. **Speed of decision-making as culture** — Rippling doesn't schedule meetings for later when a decision can be made now. If the right person isn't in the room, they Slack-call them in immediately. Deadlines for quarterly planning are firm — if you miss the window, the company moves on. This culture is modeled by CEO Parker Conrad and enforced by its own gravity as everyone internalizes the expectation.

5. **Small teams founding new products** — Rippling's model for launching new product lines: find one entrepreneurial engineer, pair them with a designer, have them spend months absorbing the platform and culture, then build with 3-4 people over 6-9 months. This pattern has been replicated a dozen times. The Time & Attendance product was built from scratch to launch by four people.

6. **Leaders are right a lot** — A Rippling leadership principle borrowed from Amazon's playbook. Product leaders' decisions spend time and energy across the entire org. The ability to enter an ambiguous situation with incomplete information and emerge with the right call is a rare, hard-to-teach skill that Rippling prizes. Equally important: great leaders change their minds when new information warrants it.

7. **Anti-framework pragmatism** — Jeremy is not against frameworks per se, but is firmly against process as a substitute for deep product thinking. The right process depends on the team's specific lifecycle. Categorizing things correctly in Jira is no substitute for understanding what needs to be built.

## Concepts that deserve their own wiki page

- **[[Compound startup]]** — Building many products on a single platform with shared data, where each new product compounds the value of the whole
- **[[Design for complexity first]]** — Inverting the MVP approach by architecting for the hardest use case so the system can gracefully handle simpler ones
- **[[Go and see]]** — Leadership principle of going all the way to ground on a problem rather than delegating understanding

## Notable quote

> "It's very, very tempting to float up here as a leader and say, 'Hey, you take that hill over there. You guys do this over here.' When in fact, where you really learn where the challenges are, or the problems or the successes is by just being there with the people in the trenches on one of the things, whichever one seems hardest or most complicated."

## B10 angle

The compound startup model is a direct counterpoint to the "do one thing well" philosophy common in early-stage advice. Jeremy's anti-MVP stance contrasts sharply with lean startup orthodoxy but aligns with the "design for the complex case" thinking seen in infrastructure-heavy products. His emphasis on [[go and see]] connects to Maya Prohovnik's [[dogfooding|maya-prohovnik]] philosophy — both argue that product leaders must have firsthand, ground-level understanding rather than delegating knowledge to specialists.
