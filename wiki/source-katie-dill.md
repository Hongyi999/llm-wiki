# Source: Quality Is Growth ft. Katie Dill

**Guest**: Katie Dill (Head of Design, Stripe; former Head of Experience Design, Airbnb; former Head of Design, Lyft)
**Published**: 2023-10-15
**Duration**: ~1h 34m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gfEEcssu304

## Executive summary

Katie Dill argues that quality and growth are not competing priorities — quality *is* growth. She shares how Stripe operationalized craft through "Walk the Store" journey reviews, a color-coded quality scoring rubric, and a formula (Performance = Potential - Interference) that has guided her team-building across three hypergrowth companies. A 10.5% revenue lift from checkout flow improvements is the proof point.

## Key ideas

1. **Quality is growth** — Dill rejects the false dichotomy between shipping features and investing in quality. At Stripe, improving the checkout experience through design details — not new features — produced a 10.5% increase in business revenue. Research found 99% of top e-commerce sites have errors in their checkout flow that hinder conversion. The growth team at Stripe is "maniacally focused on building better experiences" because they have seen quality improvements directly tied to business metrics.

2. **Walk the Store: operationalizing quality** — Stripe identified 15 essential user journeys, each owned by an engineering/product/design triad. Owners regularly friction-log these journeys from the user's perspective (starting from Google search through to dashboard), score them on a color-coded rubric (utility, usability, desirability, "surprisingly great"), then calibrate scores in Product Quality Reviews (PQRs) with Dill, CTO David Singleton, and product leaders. The process catches quality regressions, reveals cross-team dependencies, and builds shared quality standards. Quarterly cadence for formal reviews, but encouraged weekly in practice.

3. **Performance = Potential - Interference** — Dill's leadership formula learned at Airbnb. Increasing potential means hiring well and developing talent. Decreasing interference means removing organizational drag — misaligned seating, broken processes, siloed teams. At Lyft, the design team sat behind a locked door, separated from engineering and product. Decisions were made without designers, leading to wasted work. Breaking down that wall (literally and figuratively) was the highest-leverage interference reduction.

4. **Beauty builds trust** — A study from the book *Beauty* by Sagmeister and Walsh found tweets from Penn Station were more negative than tweets from Grand Central — the environment shapes human emotion. Dill extends this to product: beauty signals painstaking care, which builds trust that the company cares about details users cannot see. For a financial infrastructure company, this trust is especially critical. "Meticulous craft" is a core Stripe operating principle.

5. **The Airbnb design intervention** — One month into her Airbnb tenure, five designers staged a formal intervention with HR, reading from papers all the things Dill was doing wrong. The core message: she had not earned their trust before driving change. She shifted from "coming in swinging" to "coming in listening," and the team went from the lowest engagement scores to the best in the company within months. Lesson: you can inflict change on people, but doing it *with* them requires trust earned through listening.

6. **Reach for the stars, land on the moon** — Companies develop a fear of bold ideas as they grow, defaulting to safe incremental improvements that are easy to measure. Dill advocates for comprehensive vision work that imagines the ideal end state (Brian Chesky's 11-star experience), then working backward into shippable increments. Without the North Star, incremental work never adds up to something coherent.

7. **Hiring designers: taste and character over tools** — It is easier to teach tools and process than taste and character. Look for: (1) great judgment and natural taste, (2) humility — it means they will listen to users, (3) courage/hustle — the willingness to say "this isn't good enough" and propose something better from a blank page.

## B10 angle

Dill's "quality is growth" thesis is the design-side complement to [[tom-conrad]]'s business math formula — Conrad argues you must understand the equation; Dill argues that quality improvements are high-leverage variables within that equation (10.5% checkout revenue lift). Her Walk the Store process echoes [[lane-shackleton]]'s product development rituals at Coda — both emphasize regular cadence-based reviews that create shared accountability. The trust-through-listening lesson from the Airbnb intervention connects to [[eric-ries]]'s point about building companies where "human flourishing" is encoded structurally, not dependent on individual leadership style.
