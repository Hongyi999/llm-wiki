# Founder-led Sales

The practice of the founder personally running the sales motion through the first ~$1M of ARR, framed by [[jen-abel|Jen Abel]] not as a revenue activity but as **research** — the fastest way to find [[product-market-fit|PMF]] in B2B.

## Core claims

- **The founder is the product** in zero-to-one sales. The market is buying belief in the founder's vision as much as the software.
- **Sales is research, not revenue.** The output of founder-led sales is not dollars but understanding.
- **Don't hire salespeople until Series A.** Seed is experimentation; hiring early professionalizes a motion that doesn't yet exist.
- **Qualification is the whole game.** "Everyone says they have a bottom-of-funnel problem. It's never a bottom-of-funnel problem."

## The sales cycle (enterprise 500+)

### 1. Outreach
- **Manual 30**: find 30 target prospects by hand, spend 15–20 minutes on each note. No tooling yet.
- **Relevancy > personalization**: show you understand their problem.
- **Lead with a counterintuitive insight**, not a pitch. Example from Jen: *"zero-to-one sales talent doesn't exist."*
- **Focus on the problem**, not the solution.
- **Short enough to fit on one mobile screen** — typically ≤4 sentences.
- **Never "we're better than X"** — different, not better.
- Channel mix: email, LinkedIn DMs, cold calls, occasionally Twitter DMs.
- Tactical: use Gmail audio playback to hear how your email sounds — catches passive-aggressive tone.

### 2. Intro call
- Be **vulnerable about being early-stage**. "We're learning — tell me how this problem shows up for you."
- Don't demo on call 1. The demo is your only controlled carrot — hold it.
- Get the **second call booked live**, on a calendar, before hanging up.

### 3. Demo call
- Even then, don't show everything. Leave them wanting more.
- Avoid the "what keeps you up at night?" trap — ask concrete behavioral questions instead.

### 4. Co-authored scope
- Turn the prospect into a guide. Let them co-write the SOW.
- **40–50% of B2B SaaS deals** need to sell **services first** before the technology contract. Heretical to most investors, but:
  - Gets the logo.
  - Gets revenue (non-recurring, but real).
  - **Controls the buyer's education** — you become the vendor who writes the purchase rationale.

### 5. Procurement
- They are professional buyers. Make their job **easy**.
- Fill out their forms for them.
- Be **classifiable** — vague positioning routes you to the high-risk kitchen-sink MSA.
- **Split contracts**: a services contract + a technology contract can shorten the critical path and create onboarding momentum.

### 6. Signature
- **Know who signs** (CFO? CLO? business-unit head?).
- Pre-write the bullets that person will need to justify the signature.
- Don't do work or count revenue until the contract is signed by finance — business units can't pay unilaterally.

## Healthy benchmarks

- **Conversion rate** (outreach → meeting): **2–15%**.
- **Win rate** (meeting → close): **15–30%**.
- **Starting ACV** for seed-stage enterprise: **$50K–$200K**, leaning $50K–$100K on first contract.
- **Sales cycle**: 6–12 months typical, 20–30% longer for regulated industries, occasionally 90 days for very hot problems.

## Signals you're onto something

- Prospect wants to pull a colleague into the next call unprompted.
- They start designing the product in their head ("so could it do X?").
- They describe a **growing, widening problem**.
- They've already tried to solve it.

## Anti-signals

- "Cool, keep me posted."
- "I'll email you." (Usually means "no.")
- The prospect is a nice person who is terrible at qualifying themselves out.

## Pep talk: the compounding moat

Enterprise is brutal to enter, but compounding once inside:
- A $60K deal can become $280K within four months via cross-sell.
- As a **preferred vendor**, you're in the room for the next problem.
- Procurement will recommend you to other business units.

## Discounting

Never discount to close — you're negotiating against yourself. Only discount for a **reciprocal concession** (design partner status, case study rights, multi-year reference). Small business has more discount tolerance because the buyer is spending personal money.

## Segment differences

- **Small business**: buyer = user. Marketing game. Fast to close, high churn.
- **Enterprise**: buyer ≠ user. Procurement, legal, IT. Slow but compounding.
- **Mid-market**: the hardest — straddles both go-to-markets, often missing internal resources to deploy.
