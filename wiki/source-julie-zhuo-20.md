# How to win friends and influence decisions | Julie Zhuo — Lenny & Friends Summit 2024

**Guest**: Julie Zhuo
**Published**: 2024-11-21
**Duration**: ~0:40:00
**YouTube**: [Lenny's Podcast — Julie Zhuo (Summit talk)](https://www.lennyspodcast.com/)

## Executive summary

Julie Zhuo — co-founder/CEO of Sundial, formerly VP of Product Design at Facebook, author of *The Making of a Manager* and the *Looking Glass* newsletter — reframes management in the AI era: managing AI agents looks almost identical to managing people, so the core managerial skills (diagnosing with data, treating with design, giving feedback as a gift) get more valuable, not less. She argues that traditional role titles (PM, designer, engineer) are collapsing into "builders," and the differentiator is taste and judgment across dimensions. The talk is a practical guide to influencing decisions without authority: pick the right dimension, frame trade-offs, treat feedback as generosity.

## Key ideas

1. **Managing AI = managing people**: The best prompts, evals, and feedback loops for LLMs are structurally identical to coaching a junior report — clear goals, specific critique, iterate. Managers already good at humans have a head start; status-update managers are in trouble.
2. **The rise of the "builder"**: As tools collapse disciplinary boundaries, the useful label is "builder" — someone with taste who can ship end-to-end. Hiring and leveling frameworks built around rigid PM/design/eng tracks will mis-sort talent.
3. **Diagnose with data, treat with design**: Data tells you *what* is broken; design/judgment decides *what to do about it*. Teams that skip either half ship confidently in the wrong direction or analyze forever without acting.
4. **The willow tree and dimensionality**: Every decision has multiple dimensions (speed, quality, cost, morale). Great operators name dimensions explicitly, then pick which to optimize — a willow bends with wind rather than fighting it. Bad operators argue as if there is only one axis.
5. **Feedback as a gift, win-win framing**: Feedback is a cost to give and a gift to receive; leaders must normalize giving it generously. Frame hard conversations as win-win ("what do we both want here?") rather than zero-sum.
