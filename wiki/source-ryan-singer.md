# A better way to plan, build, and ship products | Ryan Singer (creator of "Shape Up")

**Guest**: Ryan Singer  
**Published**: 2025-03-30  
**Duration**: 1:45:10  
**YouTube**: [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GF-yUANql0c](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GF-yUANql0c)

## Executive summary

Ryan Singer, one of the earliest employees and former Head of Strategy at 37signals (makers of Basecamp), shares the Shape Up method for building software — a practical alternative to Scrum, Agile, and SAFe. The approach centers on three pillars: setting fixed time budgets (appetites) instead of estimates, collaboratively shaping solutions with engineering before committing to build, and giving teams full ownership within a time box rather than prescribing tickets. Singer emphasizes that shaping sessions — where product, design, and engineering wrestle together at a whiteboard to define a buildable idea in under ten moving pieces — are the critical differentiator that most teams skip when adopting Shape Up. He is candid about the unique advantages Basecamp had (coding designers, no sales team, founder-led decisions) and explains how his post-Basecamp consulting work has focused on closing the gaps for real-world teams that lack those conditions.

## Key ideas

1. **Appetites over estimates**: Instead of asking 'how long will this take?', Shape Up asks 'how much time are we willing to spend?' This fixed time budget forces creative trade-offs and prevents projects from ballooning, treating engineering time like a scarce budget rather than an open-ended commitment.

2. **Shaping is the make-or-break step**: A shaping session brings product, design, and a senior engineer into the same room to whiteboard a solution described in fewer than ten moving pieces. The output is not a PRD or Figma file but a clear enough diagram that any builder can say 'I know what to go build now,' and it de-risks the biggest unknowns before the time box begins.

3. **Teams own the how, not just the what**: After shaping, the build team receives a single coherent idea — not a backlog of tickets. They decompose it into their own tasks, fostering engagement and accountability. A useful kickoff exercise is breaking the shaped idea into nine major implementation scopes to confirm it fits the time box.

4. **The circuit breaker principle**: If a project is not on track to finish at the end of its time box, the team pulls it out of build mode and returns it to shaping mode rather than extending the deadline or forcing painful scope cuts. This prevents the morale-killing pattern of perpetual 'one more sprint.'

5. **Basecamp is not a template for everyone**: Singer openly acknowledges that Basecamp's advantages — coding designers, no sales org, founders embedded in product, slow deliberate hiring — are rare. His post-book work ('Shaping in Real Life') focuses on bridging the gap for teams that have walls between design and engineering, contention for engineering time, and mixed-seniority staff.

## Concepts covered

- **[[shape-up|Shape Up]]** — A product development methodology centered on fixed time budgets (appetites), collaborative shaping sessions, and team autonomy during build.
- **[[shape-up|Appetite]]** — The maximum amount of time a business is willing to invest in a project, used instead of estimation to drive scope trade-offs.
- **[[shape-up|Shaping]]** — Cross-functional work to define a buildable solution in fewer than ten moving pieces before committing engineering time.
- **[[shape-up|Circuit breaker]]** — The principle of pulling a project out of build mode and back into shaping if it is not on track at the end of its time box.
- **[[shape-up|Fat marker sketch]]** — A low-fidelity drawing technique used in shaping sessions to communicate the core idea without premature detail.
- **[[shape-up|Breadboarding]]** — A technique for mapping user flows with named components and connections to clarify functionality without visual design.
