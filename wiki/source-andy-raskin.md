# Source: The power of strategic narrative | Andy Raskin

**Guest**: Andy Raskin (strategic narrative consultant to CEOs at Salesforce, Gong, Zuora, etc.)
**Published**: 2023-05-28
**Duration**: ~1h 03m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dkVJnaxDlXE

## Executive summary

A strategic narrative is a single story about a large undeniable shift in the world that your company is uniquely positioned to help customers navigate. Andy's framework: (1) name a big relevant change, (2) show there are winners and losers, (3) tease the promised land, (4) introduce obstacles, (5) present evidence you can deliver. Unlike a positioning statement or pitch deck, the strategic narrative is a leadership alignment tool — it should power sales, marketing, product roadmap, recruiting, and fundraising simultaneously.

## Key ideas

1. **Old game → new game** — The opening move is naming an undeniable shift in the world (not a problem with your product). Zuora: "the subscription economy." This forces prospects to self-select: am I in the old game or the new game?
2. **Strategic narrative ≠ mission statement** — Mission statements are about the company; strategic narratives are about the customer's world. The company is the guide, not the hero.
3. **"The Greatest Game" framing** — The most powerful narratives make the stakes existential: if you don't adapt to this shift, you'll be left behind. Winners thrive; losers become irrelevant.
4. **CEO-level alignment tool** — The narrative must be co-created with the CEO and leadership team, not handed down by marketing. When the CEO can't tell the story with conviction, the company has a strategy problem, not a messaging problem.
5. **Promised land before product** — Describe the world the customer will inhabit (the promised land) before showing how your product gets them there. This creates desire before introducing the solution.
6. **Evidence via customer stories** — Don't prove your case with feature lists; prove it with named customers who've reached the promised land. Specific > generic.

## B9 angle

Direct counterpoint to [[source-april-dunford|Dunford]], who explicitly critiques Raskin-style "trend-first" pitches as overused and often ungrounded. Raskin's framework works best for CEO-level alignment and category-creating narratives; Dunford's works best for mid-funnel sales execution. The two are complementary at different stages of the funnel. Also complements [[source-christopher-lochhead|Lochhead's]] category design — Raskin's "name a shift" is Lochhead's "frame-name-claim" in narrative form.
