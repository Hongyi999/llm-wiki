# The art and wisdom of changing teams | Heidi Helfand (Author of Dynamic Reteaming)

**Guest**: Heidi Helfand
**Published**: 2024-01-18
**Duration**: 1:10:21
**YouTube**: [Lenny's Podcast — Heidi Helfand](https://www.lennyspodcast.com/)

## Executive summary

Heidi Helfand — author of *Dynamic Reteaming*; 20+ years as an enablement/coaching leader at fast-growing SaaS companies including ExpertCity (GoToMyPC/GoToMeeting), AppFolio, and Procore — reframes "reorgs" as dynamic reteaming: a continuous, inevitable set of patterns rather than rare top-down events. She identifies five patterns (one-by-one, grow-and-split, merging, isolation, switching), each with tactics and anti-patterns. Her through-line for leaders: stop fighting team change, involve affected people transparently (whiteboard reteaming), and treat the "people layer" as its own discipline beyond shipping product. Team stability is a myth; team redundancy and practicing change is the real skill.

## Key ideas

1. **Five patterns of reteaming**: One-by-one (joining/leaving), grow-and-split, merging, isolation (beneficial silo for innovation or emergencies), switching (moves within company for fulfillment/learning). Calling a change by the right pattern name changes how you manage it.
2. **Transparent / whiteboard reteaming**: Rather than secret back-room reorgs, Heidi advocates rolling out draft org designs on whiteboards with team names, missions, and open slots, inviting feedback. Time-box tightly so the process doesn't consume the org.
3. **Innovation by isolation works when you protect it**: For beneficial silos (startups-within-companies) to succeed: physically separate the team, explicitly tell others not to disturb them, give them process freedom and a faster cadence, ensure they report to a decision-maker who can clear obstacles. ExpertCity's GoToMyPC pivot is the canonical story.
4. **RIDE decision-making framework**: Who is Requesting the change, who gives Input, who Decides, who Executes — credited to Pat Wadors. Clarifying this up front prevents the chaos that makes reorgs feel arbitrary.
5. **Key anti-patterns**: The "percentage allocation" fallacy (splitting people across 10%/20%/30% of many projects), "poof they're gone" surprise moves without communication, and spreading high performers to "seed" other teams (Jon Walker's AppFolio experiment — kills original magic without transferring it).
