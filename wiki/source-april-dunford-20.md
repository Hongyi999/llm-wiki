# Source: How to nail your product positioning | April Dunford (Obviously Awesome)

**Guest**: [[source-april-dunford|April Dunford]] (positioning authority; author of *Obviously Awesome* and *Sales Pitch*) — earlier Lenny appearance
**Published**: 2023-01-22
**Duration**: ~1h 06m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hdjlCLb9Hl8

## Executive summary

April's foundational positioning episode (precedes the later *Sales Pitch* episode). Core thesis: positioning is the context-setting act that determines how buyers perceive your product. Most companies default to the category they started in, even when the product has evolved beyond it. The five-component positioning framework — competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, best-fit customers, market category — must be worked through in that order (not starting with category). Also: buyers need to understand market context before they can evaluate features, and 40–60% of B2B purchases end in no decision because buyers can't figure out how to choose confidently.

## Key ideas

1. **Five-component positioning framework** — (1) Competitive alternatives (what would customers use if you didn't exist?), (2) unique attributes, (3) value those attributes enable, (4) best-fit customers, (5) market category. Work them in this order; category comes last.
2. **Market context before features** — Buyers use category to set expectations. If you say "email," they expect inbox/compose/send. If you say "CRM," different expectations. Category choice determines which features seem differentiated vs. table-stakes.
3. **Positioning ≠ messaging** — Positioning is a strategic input; messaging is the creative output. You can't write good messaging on a bad positioning foundation.
4. **Help Scout case study** — Help Scout positioned as "help desk" → competed against Zendesk on features. Repositioned as "customer service for digital businesses that treat service as a growth driver" → completely different competitive set, different value story.
5. **Category creation skepticism** — Most legendary companies (Google, Facebook, Salesforce, Snowflake) did NOT create new categories; they nailed positioning within existing ones, then expanded. Category creation is risky and expensive.
6. **Teaching buyers how to buy** — Champions have never purchased your type of software. Give them a market map, purchase criteria, and cover for IT/procurement/finance objections.
