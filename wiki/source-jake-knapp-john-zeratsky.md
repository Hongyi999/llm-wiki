# Source: Making Time for What Matters — Highlights, Lasers & Design Sprints ft. Jake Knapp & John Zeratsky

**Guest**: Jake Knapp & John Zeratsky (Authors of *Sprint* and *Make Time*; Partners, Character VC)
**Published**: 2024-02-11
**Duration**: ~1h 36m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cuce7zvOFHY

## Executive summary

Jake Knapp and John Zeratsky share the framework behind their book *Make Time*, which emerged from lessons learned running structured design sprints with startups. The core thesis is that productivity isn't about getting faster at the things already in front of you — it's about changing your defaults so that the most important thing (your "highlight") gets your peak attention each day. The conversation covers their four-part system (Highlight, Laser, Energize, Reflect), with heavy emphasis on practical tactics for defeating the "busy bandwagon" and "infinity pools."

## Key ideas

1. **The Highlight principle** — Each day, ask: "If someone asked me at the end of the day what the highlight was, what would I want to say?" Then protect 60-90 minutes of peak energy for that one thing. Write it on a sticky note. The highlight can come from urgency ("this must get done today"), satisfaction ("I'd feel great finishing this"), or joy ("this would make me happy"). If you accomplish one highlight, that's a great day. If you miss it, Groundhog Day it — just repeat the same highlight tomorrow.

2. **Busy bandwagon + infinity pools = a bad flywheel** — The "busy bandwagon" is the cultural expectation that everyone should be busy, creating internal pressure to react and respond. "Infinity pools" are any app where you can pull-to-refresh or it streams endlessly (email, Twitter, TikTok, news). Together they create a vicious cycle: you feel you should be busy, and there's always an infinity pool ready to absorb that energy. The solution is to change defaults, not to rely on willpower.

3. **Willpower never wins — create barriers instead** — Jake has had no email on his phone since 2012. John stays logged out of Twitter/LinkedIn on his computer and uses a Chrome extension to disable LinkedIn's feed. John uses two-factor authentication not for security but as a speed bump against impulsive logins. Jake canceled the internet in his office so it becomes a pure focus workspace. The key insight: deleting an app doesn't delete your data; you can reinstall anytime, but the friction reminds you of your intention.

4. **Design Your Day with your calendar** — Use your calendar as a canvas for how you *want* to spend time, not as a thing done to you. John protects Monday/Wednesday/Thursday mornings for focus work. They use calendar templates at the team level with designated content buckets for different activities. Block everything — exercise, meals, even showers — so the free time is actually protected for your highlight.

5. **Reset expectations to slow the inbox** — Add an email signature: "I'm checking email once or twice a day because I'm working on an important project." The word "because" is critical — research shows that even a bogus explanation dramatically increases compliance. The real power is internal: it renegotiates your own expectation of how responsive you need to be. If you respond less frequently, the whole feedback loop slows down.

6. **Project A vs. everything else** — The things most worth doing (Project A) are big, hard to start, slow to produce dopamine, and non-urgent. The things that fill your day instead are small, obvious how to start, instant dopamine, and supposedly urgent. Even OKRs can be in the latter category. The highlight framework is designed to ensure Project A gets attention despite every default working against it.

7. **Attention residue is real** — Concept from Sophie Leroy (University of Washington): even after you stop checking an app, the thoughts stick and create mental static. Jake describes the feeling of having email on his phone as a persistent "disquiet" — once you've experienced its absence, you can never un-feel the difference. The phone-as-ring metaphor from *Lord of the Rings*: "If you've ever felt like your phone was the Ring and you wanted to put your hand in your pocket and touch it without even knowing... that's what this thing is like."

8. **Reflect as the scientific method** — The fourth step is reflecting on what worked and what didn't, with curiosity rather than self-judgment. The framework expects experimentation — Jake gives himself a "B to B-" most days, sometimes a C, sometimes an A. The 87 tactics in the book are a menu, not a mandate. Different tactics work for different people. Cycle through them, reinstall and uninstall apps as needed, adjust and retry.

## Concepts that deserve their own wiki page

- **[[Highlight principle]]** — The practice of choosing one 60-90 minute activity each day that would be the satisfying answer to "What was the highlight of your day?" as the foundation of personal productivity
- **[[Infinity pools]]** — Endlessly replenishing content sources (pull-to-refresh, streaming) that exploit the busy bandwagon to consume attention without bound
- **[[Changing defaults]]** — The productivity philosophy that willpower fails against environmental design; instead of trying harder, change the default state of your tools, devices, and calendar
- **[[Design sprints]]** — Structured five-day process for teams to design, prototype, and test ideas with real users, originated at Google Ventures

## Notable quote

> "In any given day, we're lucky if we can have one great moment where we have our peak attention and we use it well. And it's not going to happen every day, but if we have some intention around it, it can happen more often than not."

## B10 angle

The "changing defaults" philosophy echoes [[mihika-kapoor|Mihika Kapoor]]'s approach to culture-building at Figma — both argue that the environment shapes behavior more than individual willpower. The highlight principle has a surprising parallel with [[mike-maples-jr|Mike Maples Jr.]]'s concept of [[force a choice not a comparison]]: instead of trying to be productive at everything (comparison trap), you pick one thing and give it everything (force a choice). Jake's sprint methodology, while not deeply covered here, directly informed Figma's hackathon culture that Mihika describes as central to [[zero-to-one product development]].
