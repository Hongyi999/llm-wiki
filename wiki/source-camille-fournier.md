# What engineers wish PMs knew (and how great managers actually operate) | Camille Fournier

**Guest**: Camille Fournier
**Published**: 2025-01-30
**Duration**: ~1:20:00
**YouTube**: [Lenny's Podcast — Camille Fournier](https://www.lennyspodcast.com/)

## Executive summary

Camille Fournier — author of *The Manager's Path* and the new O'Reilly *Platform Engineering*, formerly CTO of Rent the Runway, MD at Goldman Sachs, and engineering leader at JP Morgan Chase and Two Sigma — argues that management is a service job, not a status promotion, and most tension between PMs and engineers comes from PMs behaving as if they own the product instead of serving the team. She lays out the specific behaviors engineers find corrosive, the trap of rewrites, and what it takes to build a durable platform team. Book frame: fewer, better one-on-ones; ruthless focus; ~10 years of IC mastery before claiming to lead.

## Key ideas

1. **What PMs do that engineers hate**: Four patterns — hoarding credit for the team's work; ignoring technical details and hand-waving as "implementation"; playing telephone between stakeholders instead of connecting them directly; hoarding ideas instead of letting the team co-create. Fixing these four unlocks most PM-eng friction.
2. **Rewrites are almost always a trap**: Engineers pitch rewrites because the old system is ugly; the rewrite usually ships later, buggier, without the features customers actually wanted. Leaders must force the hard conversation: what user problem does this solve, and can we do it incrementally?
3. **Management is a service job**: The manager's job is to make the team effective, not to be the smartest person in the room. That means fewer one-on-ones (not more), ruthless prioritization, and actively resisting the pull to solve problems yourself — which steals growth from reports.
4. **~10 years of IC mastery before management**: Camille is skeptical of fast-tracking ICs. Real technical judgment takes roughly a decade of shipping. Premature managers become process-pushers who can't evaluate the work.
5. **Platform teams need SWE + SRE + PM**: Platform engineering fails when staffed only with infrastructure engineers. You need software engineers (build tools), SREs (operate them), and product managers (prioritize internal customers). Start a platform team around 50+ engineers, not earlier.
