# Translating vision into goals | Nickey Skarstad

**Guest**: Nickey Skarstad
**Published**: 2022-07-18
**Duration**: ~1:00:00 (audio-only episode; see Spotify)
**Spotify**: [open.spotify.com/episode/6FKUgLvnlpyLOyMbykXm6u](https://open.spotify.com/episode/6FKUgLvnlpyLOyMbykXm6u)

## Executive summary

Nickey Skarstad (Director of Product, Duolingo; ex-Airbnb Experiences, Etsy, Shopify, The Wing) brings rare cross-company tour-of-duty insight to the gap between product vision and weekly execution. Her core contribution: treating product quality as an operational metric — not a vibe — and designing the rituals and metrics that make quality self-sustaining. She also articulates the "second-order decisions" frame: most PM decisions are cheap, but a few cascade for years, and the job is knowing which is which.

## Key ideas

1. **Operationalize product quality**: Quality drifts when no one owns a quality metric. Name one (NPS, qualitative rubric, returning-user NPS). Review it weekly. Escalate regressions. Without a metric, quality becomes whoever-complains-loudest.
2. **Vision → goals → execution cascade**: Vision is the 5-year aspiration. Goals are the 12-month outcomes that make vision real. Execution is the 6-week bets that drive goals. Most teams skip the middle layer and get random work.
3. **Second-order decisions deserve disproportionate time**: Most PM decisions have small future impact. A few (org structure, foundational metrics, who to hire) cascade for years. Slow down for second-order decisions even if the first-order pressure is "just decide."
4. **Brainstorming needs structure**: Open-ended brainstorms produce consensus mush. Effective brainstorms set the prompt tightly ("ten ways to reduce activation friction in the first minute"), time-box individual ideation before group discussion, and separate generation from evaluation.
5. **Influence without authority**: Influence is the PM's primary tool. Build it by consistently being right about things others were wrong about, by making other people's work visibly better, and by being the first to say "I was wrong."
