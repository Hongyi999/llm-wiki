# Lessons from scaling Uber and Opendoor | Brian Tolkin (Head of Product at Opendoor, ex-Uber)

**Guest**: Brian Tolkin  
**Published**: 2024-08-04  
**Duration**: 1:14:40  
**YouTube**: [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sRukk520Fds](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sRukk520Fds)

## Executive summary

Brian Tolkin, Head of Product and Design at Opendoor and former employee #100 at Uber, shares lessons from building products with heavy operational components. Starting on Uber's ops team and moving into product gave him deep customer empathy and a unique understanding of where technology leverage matters most. He describes Uber's early days as a 'twin turbine jet plane' where product and ops must work in harmony, explains how he formalized the product operations function, and details how Opendoor applies jobs-to-be-done thinking to a business where customers transact once every seven years. The conversation also covers how to run effective product reviews (accountability plus helping teams think better), experimentation with low sample sizes, staying calm under pressure, and the story of Zillow's failed attempt to compete with Opendoor's vertically integrated model.

## Key ideas

1. **Product and ops as twin turbines**: In operations-heavy tech companies, product and ops must operate like a twin-turbine jet plane—you can fly on one engine briefly, but peak performance requires both. Ops teams iterate faster and have qualitative insights; product teams scale solutions. Mutual respect and a strong bidirectional feedback loop are essential.

2. **Do things that don't scale, then scale them**: Uber's driver onboarding evolved from 90-minute one-on-one sessions to classrooms to videos to OCR-based automation. The pattern is to iterate manually at small scale, find what works, then invest technology where leverage is highest—and free up ops teams for the next frontier.

3. **Product reviews should help, not intimidate**: Effective product reviews balance accountability/informing with helping teams think through problems. Leaders should probe and ask questions rather than mandate, recognize the team has 40-60 hours/week of depth on the problem, and use flash tags (FYI, suggestion, plea) to signal how strongly they feel.

4. **Jobs to be done as cultural DNA, not rigid template**: At Opendoor, JTBD is used flexibly to force customer empathy in a business where employees rarely buy/sell homes themselves. The real value is cultural internalization—asking 'what is the actual job to be done?' and distinguishing between the customer's job (price discovery) and the business's framing (get an Opendoor offer).

5. **Build conviction creatively when A/B testing is limited**: With low transaction volumes, run power analysis honestly, accept 6-month experiment runtimes when needed, reduce confidence thresholds (80% vs 95%), use diff-in-diff or geo-segmentation, talk to more customers, and ultimately trust intuition when data isn't available—but maintain a feedback loop.

## Concepts covered

- **[[product-operating-model|Product Operations]]** — A function Brian helped formalize at Uber that bridges centralized product teams and distributed ops teams through bidirectional feedback loops.
- **Twin Turbine Model** — A metaphor for product-ops harmony: both engines must work together for peak efficiency, though you can fly on one briefly.
- **[[jobs-to-be-done|Jobs to Be Done]]** — A framework for understanding what customers are actually trying to accomplish, applied flexibly at Opendoor to force empathy in a low-frequency transaction business.
- **Vertical Integration** — Opendoor's strategy of owning pricing, product, operations, risk, and capital markets end-to-end—which proved too complex for Zillow to replicate.
- **Competition-Aware Not Competition-Focused** — The principle of staying focused on your customer rather than obsessing over competitors, especially when the total addressable market is much larger than current penetration.
