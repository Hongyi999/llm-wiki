# Source: Building deeper, more robust relationships ft. Carole Robin

**Guest**: Carole Robin (Former Stanford GSB Professor; Founder, Leaders in Tech)
**Published**: 2024-04-25
**Duration**: ~1h 27m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cew9-GlC_yk

## Executive summary

Carole Robin spent 20+ years teaching Stanford GSB's legendary "Touchy Feely" course (formally Interpersonal Dynamics) and distills its core lessons: relationships exist on a continuum from "contact, no connection" to "exceptional," and the skills to move along that continuum are learnable. Her framework centers on progressive disclosure (the 15% rule), staying on your side of the net (the three realities), and understanding that anger is almost always a secondary emotion masking fear or hurt.

## Key ideas

1. **The 15% rule** — Step just 15% outside your comfort zone in disclosure and vulnerability. This sits in the "learning zone" between your comfort zone and your danger zone. You will feel slightly uncomfortable but won't freak yourself or the other person out. Each successful disclosure creates a new, larger comfort zone from which you can go 15% further. This is how relationships deepen incrementally.

2. **The three realities and "stay on your side of the net"** — In any exchange, there are three realities: (1) my intent, (2) the observable behavior, and (3) the impact on you. Each person is privy to only two. Conflict erupts when we assume we know the other person's reality. The feedback formula: "When you do [behavior], I feel [emotion from feelings vocabulary], and I'm telling you because [desired outcome]." Saying "I feel that you don't care" is not a feeling — it is an attribution that guarantees defensiveness.

3. **Anger is a secondary emotion** — Under anger there is almost always fear, hurt, or sadness. Anger is a distancing emotion; fear, hurt, and sadness are connecting emotions. A founder who replaced "I'm furious you missed this deadline" with "I'm afraid I'm the only one here who is as concerned about this" had his team rally to fix the problem faster than ever before.

4. **Mental models limit us** — We form beliefs early ("leave feelings in the parking lot") that serve us initially and then over-serve us. Robin learned this when a direct report asked, "Are you human after all?" — the moment she became a leader. Leaders who won't update their mental models can't inspire others to update theirs. Carol Dweck's "yet" is an instant mental model update.

5. **Appropriate vulnerability makes leaders stronger** — This is Robin's contrarian view. A leader who stands up and says "I don't have the answer and I've never needed you all more" is stronger than one who pretends nothing is wrong. Referent power — being someone others say "When I grow up, I want to be more like that" — is the most sustainable form of leadership.

6. **Feedback builds relationships** — People believe giving feedback will ruin relationships; actually, feedback poorly given ruins relationships. Feedback should be constructive (problem-solving) or complimentary (specific appreciation, not "nice job"). Address pinches before they become crunches. Substitute "I" for "it" in "it's not worth it" — "I'm not worth it" makes the stakes clearer.

7. **Advice hinders; inquiry helps** — Leaders often believe they must have all the answers. Giving advice creates power differentials and enables dependency. Instead, be a thought partner: "What have you already considered? Where are you stuck?" Use artful inquiry — open questions starting with what, when, where, how. Avoid why (triggers defensiveness) and yes/no questions (limiting).

## B11 angle

Robin's course is explicitly the complement to [[jeffrey-pfeffer]]'s "Paths to Power" — together they form the two most popular Stanford GSB classes, one teaching the external game of influence and the other the internal game of connection. Her 15% rule parallels [[graham-weaver]]'s "worse first" principle — both acknowledge that growth requires deliberate discomfort. The emphasis on vulnerability and asking for help directly connects to [[phyl-terry]]'s thesis that asking for help is a giving activity, not a sign of weakness. Robin's feedback framework offers the interpersonal skills that make [[jeff-weinstein]]'s customer obsession and [[jess-lachs]]'s extreme ownership culture sustainable at scale.
