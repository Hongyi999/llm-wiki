# Counter-Positioning

Counter-positioning occurs when a new entrant adopts a business model or approach that an incumbent cannot copy without damaging its existing business. It is one of the seven powers in [[source-hamilton-helmer|Hamilton Helmer]]'s strategy framework and is independently validated by [[source-roger-martin|Roger Martin]]'s strategy work, making it the only strategy concept that appears in both canonical strategy interviews in B3.

## The mechanism

Helmer defines counter-positioning as the **starting refuge** for startups: the incumbent sees the new approach, understands it, but rationally declines to copy it because doing so would cannibalize its existing revenue or destroy its organizational identity. The barrier is not ignorance but rational self-interest.

Classic examples:
- **Netflix vs. Blockbuster**: Blockbuster understood streaming but couldn't abandon $800M in late fees
- **Vanguard vs. active managers**: Index funds were obvious but would destroy fee structures
- **Tesla vs. traditional auto**: EVs required abandoning combustion engine supply chains and dealer networks

## Helmer's framing (7 Powers)

In the [[seven-powers|7 Powers framework]], counter-positioning is the first power a startup should seek. The natural progression is:

1. **Counter-positioning** — your starting refuge from incumbents
2. **Scale economies** — as you grow, unit costs decline
3. **Switching costs** — customers become locked in
4. **Network economies** — value increases with participation

Counter-positioning provides time — the incumbent's rational inaction creates a window for the entrant to build other powers.

## Martin's framing (Playing to Win)

Roger Martin approaches the same phenomenon through his **Playing to Win** framework. He argues that great strategy requires making **choices that competitors find uncomfortable to mirror** — not because they can't see the move, but because matching it would require abandoning their existing where-to-play and how-to-win decisions. His canonical example is Procter & Gamble under A.G. Lafley choosing to invest disproportionately in Tide at the premium end, a move competitors couldn't match without conceding other segments.
