# Source: Jen Abel — The Ultimate Guide to Founder-Led Sales

Lenny's Podcast interview with [[jen-abel|Jen Abel]], co-founder of [[jjellyfish|JJELLYFISH]], which embeds with founders to teach enterprise sales. Published 2024-11-24. Jen makes the case that **founder-led sales is research, not revenue**, and that it's the fastest way to find [[product-market-fit|product-market fit]] in B2B.

## Core thesis

**The founder is the product** in the earliest sales motion. You cannot outsource the first million of ARR because selling *is* learning what to build. Jen recommends not hiring any salespeople until Series A — seed is about experimentation, not exploitation. See [[founder-led-sales]].

## Cold outreach principles

- **Relevancy > personalization**. A note that shows you understand their problem beats a note that mentions their dog.
- **Lead with a counterintuitive insight**, not a pitch. Example Jen used: *"zero-to-one sales talent doesn't exist."*
- **Focus on the problem, not the solution.** Don't even describe your product on first touch.
- **Short enough that it fits on one mobile screen** with no scroll. Typically ≤4 sentences.
- **Never use "we're better than X."** Procurement professionals will kill you for it. Be *different*, not *better*.
- Tactical trick: read your email out loud in Gmail's audio playback — you'll hear when it sounds passive-aggressive.

## The manual 30

Before touching any sales tool, **manually find 30 target prospects** and spend 15–20 minutes writing each one a thoughtful note. This answers three questions:
1. Are they even discoverable? (If no, your ICP is undefined.)
2. Do they share commonalities? (Team size, industry, tenure — parameters emerge.)
3. Does anyone respond? (The signal.)

Only after the manual 30 should you think about Clay or any enrichment tool. Volume without parameters is spam.

## The sales cycle (enterprise, 500+ employees)

1. **Intro call** — be vulnerable about being early-stage. "We're learning — can you tell me how this problem shows up for you?" Don't demo on call 1. The demo is the only carrot you fully control, so hold it.
2. **Demo call** — even then, don't show everything. Leave them wanting more.
3. **Co-authored scope** — turn the prospect into a guide. Let them co-write the SOW. 40–50% of B2B SaaS deals need to sell **services first** before the technology contract. This is heresy to most investors but it gets the logo, the revenue, and — crucially — control of the buyer's education.
4. **Procurement** — they are professional buyers. Make their job easy. Fill out their forms for them. Be classifiable (vague positioning gets you routed to the high-risk kitchen-sink MSA). Consider splitting into a services contract + a technology contract to shorten the critical path.
5. **Signature** — know *who* signs (CFO? CLO? business-unit head?). Pre-write the bullets that person will need to justify the signature. Jen lost a month on a pharma deal because the CFO got the contract with no context and kicked it back.

## Qualification is the whole game

> "Everyone says they have a bottom-of-funnel problem. It's never a bottom-of-funnel problem. It's always a top-of-funnel problem."

- **Conversion rate** (outreach → meeting): healthy range **2–15%**.
- **Win rate** (meeting → close): healthy range **15–30%**.
- If win rate is low, the answer is almost never "better closing skills" — it's wrong persona, wrong message, or wrong problem. Insight trumps tactics.
- Tactical counter-advice to the usual "what keeps you up at night?" — don't ask it. The answer changes daily. Ask concrete behavioral questions: "how are you measuring this today? who did you hire to own it? what have you already tried?"

## Signals you're onto something

- The prospect wants to pull another person into the next call.
- They spontaneously start designing the product in their head: "so could it do X?"
- They describe a **growing, widening problem** — not a static annoyance.
- They've already tried to solve it (headcount, another tool, an internal project).

Conversely: if every call ends with "cool, keep me posted," you're in the friend zone. (This echoes Rick Song's "friend zone test" mentioned by [[todd-jackson|Todd Jackson]].)

## Pricing, discounting, and ACV

- Starting ACV sweet spot for seed-stage enterprise: **$50K–$200K**, leaning **$50K–$100K** on first contract.
- Discounting to close is **negotiating against yourself**. Only discount for a reciprocal concession (design partner status, multi-year reference, case study).
- Small business is a marketing game (volume, velocity). Enterprise is a craft (relationships, cycles). Mid-market straddles both and is the hardest.

## Pep talk

Enterprise is brutal to enter but compounding once inside. A $60K deal can become $280K in four months via cross-sell. Being a preferred vendor means you're in the room when the next problem gets discussed — that's the real moat.
