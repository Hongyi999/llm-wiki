# Synthesis: B9 — Sales, GTM & pricing convergent patterns

8 patterns that emerge across 19 unique B9 sources. Each is independently articulated by 3+ voices and represents a point of genuine convergence (not forced agreement).

## Pattern 1: Buyers are afraid, not uninterested

The deepest convergence in B9. Dixon's data (56% of pipeline losses = buyer indecision, not status-quo preference) is independently echoed by Dunford (40–60% of deals die to indecision), Grosser (80% buy to avoid pain/risk), and Abel (champion career-risk is the real gate). **Implication**: the job of a sales motion is risk reduction, not urgency creation.

**Voices**: [[source-matt-dixon|Dixon]], [[source-april-dunford|Dunford]], [[source-jeanne-grosser|Grosser]], [[source-jen-abel-20|Abel]]

## Pattern 2: Founders must sell first

Every sales-focused B9 voice agrees: outsourcing sales before the founder has personally closed 20–50 customers breaks the feedback loop needed for product and positioning refinement. Kazanjy provides the metrics (15–25% win rate threshold); Abel provides the qualitative frame (each objection is a positioning data point); Butler shows Figma doing it with Dylan DMing designers in Lyfts.

**Voices**: [[source-pete-kazanjy|Kazanjy]], [[source-jen-abel-20|Abel]], [[source-claire-butler|Butler]]

## Pattern 3: Product must eventually own pipeline

The PLS pattern: as companies mature past founder-led sales, product must take ownership of pipeline creation and monetization KPIs. Verna makes this structural (product owns PQA targets); Butler shows Figma's implementation (designer advocates, not sales, drove enterprise adoption); Dietrich validates at Atlassian (2–3x R&D vs. sales spend).

**Voices**: [[source-elena-verna-20|Verna]], [[source-claire-butler|Butler]], [[source-carilu-dietrich|Dietrich]], [[source-raaz-herzberg|Herzberg]]

## Pattern 4: Pricing is a product decision

Ramanujam (pricing must be cross-functional from day one), Butler (Figma's collaborator-limit → file-limit pivot), Schottenstein (pricing changes deserve same rigor as product launches), and Grosser (treat GTM itself as a product) all converge: pricing decisions shape adoption trajectories and competitive dynamics. They are product strategy, not finance arithmetic.

**Voices**: [[source-madhavan-ramanujam-20|Ramanujam]], [[source-claire-butler|Butler]], [[source-julia-schottenstein|Schottenstein]], [[source-jeanne-grosser|Grosser]]

## Pattern 5: Teach the buyer how to buy

Dunford: give the champion a market map and purchase criteria. Dixon: make a concrete recommendation (delegation effect). Abel: pre-load champions with answers for IT/procurement/finance. Grosser: the first meeting should be a whiteboarding session, not a discovery quiz. The buyer has never purchased this type of software before — help them.

**Voices**: [[source-april-dunford|Dunford]], [[source-matt-dixon|Dixon]], [[source-jen-abel-20|Abel]], [[source-jeanne-grosser|Grosser]]

## Pattern 6: Do things that don't scale, then systematize

Butler: Dylan drove to Palo Alto to fix a designer's MacBook. Becker: turned a taxi conversation with Uber's co-founder into a 10-year agency relationship. Abel: enterprise deals are "co-authored via text" with a champion. Kazanjy: "while loop" — run the motion locally, debug, then replicate. **The manual version must work before the automated version can.**

**Voices**: [[source-claire-butler|Butler]], [[source-jonathan-becker|Becker]], [[source-jen-abel-20|Abel]], [[source-pete-kazanjy|Kazanjy]]

## Pattern 7: Free tier design determines growth trajectory

Figma's pivot from collaborator-limit to file-limit (Butler), Verna's monetization-awareness hierarchy (most users don't know what they're buying), Wiz's free individual scans → team → enterprise (Herzberg), and Schottenstein's open-source-to-enterprise arc all converge: the free tier is not a marketing tactic but a structural growth decision. Get it wrong and you strangle your own growth engine.

**Voices**: [[source-claire-butler|Butler]], [[source-elena-verna-20|Verna]], [[source-raaz-herzberg|Herzberg]], [[source-julia-schottenstein|Schottenstein]]

## Pattern 8: Positioning is upstream of everything

Whether expressed as Dunford's five-component framework, Raskin's strategic narrative, Lochhead's category design, or Grosser's GTM-as-product, every B9 voice agrees that positioning (or its equivalent) is the foundational strategic act. Bad positioning produces bad pitches, bad pricing, bad sales motions, and bad marketing. Fix positioning first.

**Voices**: [[source-april-dunford|Dunford]], [[source-april-dunford-20|Dunford (earlier)]], [[source-andy-raskin|Raskin]], [[source-christopher-lochhead|Lochhead]], [[source-jeanne-grosser|Grosser]]

## Meta-pattern: The GTM maturity arc

B9 collectively describes a maturity arc:

| Stage | Motion | Key B9 voice |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-PMF | Founder sells personally | Kazanjy, Abel (1st) |
| Post-PMF, pre-scale | Bottom-up IC adoption, free tier design | Butler, Verna, Herzberg |
| Scaling | Product-led sales, designer/practitioner advocates | Verna, Butler, Dietrich |
| Enterprise | Pitch architecture, champion arming, de-risking | Dunford, Dixon, Abel (2nd) |
| Mature | GTM-as-product, AI-native ops, segmentation | Grosser, Ramanujam |
| Downturn | Retention-first, comp redesign, conservative planning | Mansuri |

This arc is the B9 analog of B7's four-lever meta-model (acquisition → activation → retention → monetization), applied to the sales/GTM domain.
