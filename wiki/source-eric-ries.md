# Source: Reflections on a Movement ft. Eric Ries

**Guest**: Eric Ries (Creator of Lean Startup methodology; Founder & Executive Chairman, Long-Term Stock Exchange)
**Published**: 2023-10-29
**Duration**: ~2h 14m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xzebbzIntFc

## Executive summary

Eric Ries reflects on 12+ years since *The Lean Startup*, addressing persistent misconceptions (MVP does not mean low quality; lean does not mean cheap), sharing what he got wrong in the book, and arguing that the next frontier is corporate governance — embedding human flourishing into company structures so that organizations remain trustworthy even after founders depart.

## Key ideas

1. **MVP is about learning efficiency, not low quality** — MVP means finding the most efficient way to test your riskiest hypothesis. In high-stakes industries (jet engines, data centers, cancer therapies), the bar is high but the logic is the same: do one thing extraordinarily well rather than 20 things half-baked. LTSE's own MVP took 10 years to build. The IMVU "teleportation" story illustrates the point: an ugly hack where avatars jumped rather than walked was perceived by customers as more advanced than competitors.

2. **What he got wrong in the book** — Two regrets: (1) the chapter on scaling Lean Startup made it sound easy and breezy, when it took an entire subsequent book (*The Startup Way*) to properly address; (2) he wrote "tools to change the world" without specifying "for the better" — he assumed that was obvious, and it turned out not to be.

3. **Pivot = change in strategy, not change in vision** — Ries defines pivot as "one foot anchored, one foot moving" (from basketball). The vision often evolves through self-discovery during building. His own whiteboard story proves the point: years later, he found a preserved whiteboard in his handwriting showing he had advocated for ideas he now remembers opposing — his vision evolved without him consciously noticing.

4. **The zombie company is worse than failure** — Building a company that won't die but that you hate is far worse than a clean failure. Ries argues for psychological dis-identification from the company so founders can let it die when necessary. Practical advice for stuck founders: give yourself a fixed time (even one weekend) to try the new thing; go around the room and have everyone say what they secretly wish the company was doing.

5. **From insurgency to obvious in one generation** — The Lean Startup went from people yelling at Ries in meetings ("that's impossible") to new founders saying "it seems obvious" within five years. Victory looks not like a parade but like the next generation taking the vocabulary for granted. The movement won not by defeating opponents but by default — almost no one defended the old Stage-Gate approach.

6. **Companies are alive — governance must encode values structurally** — Organizations are superorganisms; no individual can make promises on their behalf. Companies are reliably good at things they structurally care about (filing quarterly reports: 100% certainty) and unreliable at things left to individual goodwill (not causing harm: "probably"). Ries advocates for structural instruments: board mission pledges, LTSPVs, public benefit corps, and foundations — warning that "it's always too early until it's too late" to set these up.

7. **AI as a management technology** — AI is fundamentally a management technology. Ries's experiments with AI agents reveal the same pathologies as human middle management: passing the buck, spawning sub-tasks infinitely, summarizing without doing. The upside: AI will democratize organizational summarization, reducing the need for hierarchical management layers. The framework for ethical AI action: choose actions today that make sense across the widest range of possible futures.

## Concepts that deserve their own wiki page

- **[[MVP (Minimum Viable Product)]]** — Not about low quality or cheapness; the most efficient way to test your riskiest hypothesis, scaled to context
- **[[Pivot]]** — A change in strategy without a change in vision; a process of self-discovery where founders learn what they actually believe
- **[[Innovation accounting]]** — Establishing a baseline of how bad the current situation is, then measuring whether experiments improve it
- **[[Corporate governance as competitive advantage]]** — Structurally embedding human flourishing into organizations so values survive beyond any individual leader

## Notable quote

> "People act like having a startup fail is the worst thing that can happen to you. And man, that's not even in the top 10. It's bad, I've done it, it's awful. But far worse is to be in a company that won't die, a zombie, undead company that you hate, but you can't leave."

## B10 angle

Ries's insistence that companies are fundamentally "math equations" — with the MVP as a tool to validate whether the equation works — directly complements [[tom-conrad]]'s "business math formula" insight. Where Conrad discovered the equation from the operator's chair at age 53, Ries baked it into a methodology a decade earlier. Ries's argument that craft without validated learning is wasted connects to [[katie-dill]]'s "quality is growth" thesis — both agree quality matters, but Ries would add that quality must be defined by the customer, not by the builder's aesthetic preference.
