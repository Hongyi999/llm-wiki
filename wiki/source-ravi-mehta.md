# How to build your product strategy stack | Ravi Mehta (Tinder, Facebook, Tripadvisor, Outpace)

**Guest**: Ravi Mehta  
**Published**: 2023-01-19

## Executive summary

Ravi Mehta, former CPO of Tinder and product leader at Facebook and Tripadvisor, introduces two powerful frameworks for product managers: the Product Strategy Stack and the PM Competency Model. The strategy stack decomposes the vague concept of 'strategy' into five clearly defined layers — company mission, company strategy, product strategy, product roadmap, and product goals — and controversially places goals after roadmap rather than before. Mehta illustrates this with a detailed Tinder vs. Hinge comparison showing how similar products diverge dramatically when driven by different missions. He also shares a 12-competency framework for PM career development and introduces the concept of 'selective micromanagement' as a legitimate leadership tool. The episode is rich with practical Tinder product discovery stories, including how 'whale' user research led to Tinder Platinum.

## Key ideas

1. **The Product Strategy Stack**: A five-layer system — company mission, company strategy, product strategy, product roadmap, product goals — that helps PMs decompose strategy into clearly defined parts. When PMs can't prioritize, the root cause is usually a gap higher up the stack, and the system can be debugged bottom-to-top.

2. **Goals come after roadmap, not before**: Controversially, Mehta places goals at the bottom of the strategy stack. Starting with goals (e.g., 'increase retention by X%') puts energy on moving metrics without structure. Instead, define your destination first, then use goals to measure whether you're reaching it — like knowing you're driving to Vegas before caring about the 250-mile odometer reading.

3. **Frontier of understanding for goal-setting**: Teams should set goals based on where their understanding frontier sits. If you don't know what moves a metric, set understanding goals. If you know the levers but can't execute, set execution goals. Only commit to outcome goals when you have both understanding and execution confidence. This prevents the common failure of committing to metrics you don't know how to move.

4. **Velocity vs. latency in startups**: Startups don't have speed advantage in velocity (quantity of work) — big companies always outproduce them. Their advantage is latency: the time from idea to validated learning. A good test is asking how long it takes to change button text and measure the result.

5. **Selective micromanagement as legitimate leadership**: The ideal is scalable leadership (high confidence, team autonomy), but when a leader lacks confidence in a team's direction, the right answer is temporary, tactical micromanagement to set the team on track — not hands-off leadership that lets them drift. The failure mode is 'micro-mismanagement' where neither leader nor team feels in control.

## Concepts covered

- **[[product-operating-model|Product Strategy Stack]]** — A five-layer framework (mission → company strategy → product strategy → roadmap → goals) for decomposing strategy into clearly defined, debuggable parts.
- **Frontier of Understanding** — The boundary between what a team knows and doesn't know, which should determine whether to set understanding, execution, or outcome goals.
- **Velocity vs. Latency** — The distinction between quantity of work (velocity, where big companies win) and speed from idea to validated learning (latency, where startups win).
- **Selective Micromanagement** — A temporary, tactical leadership mode where a leader intervenes in detail to correct a team's direction, with the explicit goal of returning to scalable, hands-off leadership.
- **PM Competency Model** — A 12-competency framework across four areas (product execution, customer insight, product strategy, leadership) for evaluating and developing product managers at any level.
- **Exponential Feedback** — Feedback that targets underlying behaviors rather than surface symptoms, creating compounding returns as the recipient builds self-diagnostic capabilities.
