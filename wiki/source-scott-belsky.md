# Source: Lessons on product sense, AI, the first mile experience, and the messy middle | Scott Belsky (Adobe)

**Guest**: Scott Belsky
**Published**: 2023-05-18
**Duration**: 1:02:33
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HCKosdV1J-8

## Executive summary

Scott Belsky, Adobe's Chief Strategy Officer and Behance founder, delivers a masterclass in product sense rooted in human psychology. He argues that the 'first mile' of any product experience — the first 30 seconds where users are 'lazy, vain, and selfish' — is the single highest-leverage area for product teams, yet consistently receives the least investment. Belsky introduces the concept of the 'golden gut' (experienced intuition for micro-decisions in product design) and makes the case that AI will 'collapse the stack' across organizations, empowering individuals to do more cross-functional work and explore 10X the surface area of creative possibilities. Drawing on lessons from building and killing features at Behance, he advocates radical reductionism — doing half the features, offering half the options — and shares his conviction-based framework for knowing when to persist through the messy middle versus when to quit.

## Key ideas

1. **The First Mile Experience**: In the first 30 seconds of using a product, users are lazy, vain, and selfish — they want instant success with zero learning curve. Most teams spend the final mile of development considering the first mile of user experience. Getting more customers through the top of funnel is the highest-leverage product work.
2. **Radical reductionism — do half**: From Behance's experience of killing features (Tip Exchange, groups, portfolio color customization) and seeing core metrics skyrocket each time, Belsky learned that teams should plan to build half the features they think they need. Optimize for the problems you want to have (users asking for more), not the problems you don't.
3. **AI collapses the organizational stack**: AI will reduce the 'game of operator' across organizations, empowering individuals to answer their own data questions, explore more design possibilities, and do cross-functional work. This levels the playing field toward small-team advantages of flatness and speed.
4. **The conviction test for the messy middle**: When founders ask whether to persist or quit, Belsky asks one question: knowing everything you now know, do you have more or less conviction than when you started? More conviction means stay; less conviction means pivot. Don't make this decision on a bad day, but if conviction generally dissipates, be open to other options.
5. **Surprise drives virality, not quality**: People don't talk about products doing what they expected — they talk about unexpected features and Easter eggs. Product teams should optimize for surprise and delight moments that users will share, not just functional completeness.

## Concepts & frameworks

- **First Mile Experience** — The onboarding, orientation, and defaults of a product's initial experience where users are 'lazy, vain, and selfish' and have zero tolerance for friction or learning curves.
- [[taste-as-differentiator|Golden Gut]] — The experienced product intuition for micro-decisions in design — knowing when presumptuous defaults beat explicit choices, when to trade 10% confusion for 90% speed — developed through empathy and shoulder-to-shoulder customer observation.
- **Collapse the Stack** — Belsky's prediction that AI will flatten organizational hierarchies by empowering individuals to perform cross-functional tasks (data analysis, design, engineering) that previously required specialists.
- **The Messy Middle** — The volatile, anonymous, uncertain years between a venture's exciting launch and its eventual success or failure, where endurance and short-circuiting the reward system are critical.
- **Optimize for Problems You Want** — When deciding MVP features, only build what prevents users from reaching the point where they care enough to ask for more — those feature requests are the problems you want to have.
- **Resourcefulness over Resources** — In constrained environments, refactoring and innovation build lasting muscle, while throwing resources at problems builds dependency — the best companies are built in resource-scarce eras.
