# Dollar-driven Customer Discovery

[[todd-jackson|Todd Jackson]]'s discipline for validating [[product-market-fit|PMF]]: **trust dollars committed, not words spoken.** Interest is cheap. Dollars are expensive. Only dollars survive the "happy ears" problem.

## The core claim

Founders routinely mistake **enthusiastic conversation** for real pull. Users want to be nice. Advisors want to encourage. Investors want their bets to work. All of them will say "this is great" without meaning "I would pay for it."

A **dollar** — in the form of a signed contract, an LOI with a price, a pre-payment, a paid pilot, or a serious procurement request — is the first signal that survives social pressure.

## The "happy ears" trap

- Founders hear what they want.
- Prospects say what sounds supportive.
- The gap between those two is where most pre-PMF startups die slowly.

Counter-tactics:
- **Write it down**: transcribe the exact words the prospect used, don't summarize optimistically.
- **Ask dollar-committing questions**: "If I put a contract in front of you this week, what would need to be true to sign it?"
- **Ask the [[madhavan-three-prices|Madhavan Ramanujam three-price question]]**: what price feels fair, expensive-but-worth-it, and prohibitive? (See below.)

## Madhavan Ramanujam's three-price question

1. At what price would you consider this a **bargain**?
2. At what price would you start to find it **expensive**, but still buy?
3. At what price would it become **prohibitive**?

The gaps between the three answers reveal how elastic the prospect is and whether a real willingness-to-pay exists. Flat answers ("I don't know") signal weak demand.

## Wow statements

Complement the dollar test with qualitative signal:
- "I would cry if you took this away."
- "This is the only tool I open when I start my day."
- "I've already told five people about it."
Spontaneous superlatives with specificity. Generic "it's great" does not count.

## The marginal customer

Related but distinct: **look at your 10 most recent customers, not your first 10**. First customers are selected on founder relationship and enthusiasm — they commit dollars out of loyalty. The marginal customer reveals whether the motion works on strangers.

## The friend-zone test

Rick Song (Persona), via Todd Jackson: if every prospect call ends with **"this is cool, keep me posted,"** you are in the friend zone, not in PMF. The friend-zone phrase is a polite rejection dressed as encouragement.

## Overlap with founder-led sales

[[jen-abel|Jen Abel]]'s [[founder-led-sales|founder-led sales]] teaching is the same idea in a different key: the output of founder sales is **learning**, and the thing that separates learning from wishful thinking is a real commitment (even a services-first commitment, not yet a technology contract). Both frameworks treat **actions, not words** as the only PMF-relevant data.
