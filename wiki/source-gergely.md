# Source: Leaving big tech to build the #1 tech newsletter ft. Gergely Orosz

**Guest**: Gergely Orosz (Founder, The Pragmatic Engineer)
**Published**: 2022-11-17
**Duration**: ~1h 15m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CtB0O0M7YS0

## Executive summary

Gergely Orosz shares the full story of leaving a $330K/year engineering manager role at Uber to build The Pragmatic Engineer, now the #1 technology newsletter on Substack with 189,000+ subscribers. The episode is a candid exploration of the creator-newsletter life: the surprising economics, the loneliness, the productivity hacks, and the years of accidental groundwork (six years of blogging) that made "overnight" success possible.

## Key ideas

1. **The accidental on-ramp** -- Gergely didn't plan to become a newsletter writer. He left Uber intending to start a VC-backed startup, got sidetracked writing books that made $100K in the first year, then realized via the "Mexican fisherman" parable that the startup path would just lead him back to writing. He committed to six months of newsletter writing as an experiment.

2. **Six years of blogging as groundwork** -- Before the newsletter launched, Gergely had been blogging at pragmaticengineer.com since ~2015. Several posts went viral on Hacker News. By launch day, he had a built-in audience who already trusted his writing. Jeff Atwood's advice: "Write a blog post, do it three times a week, do it for two years, and you'll be famous."

3. **Deadlines create discipline** -- The biggest productivity challenge of solo work is unstructured time. Gergely's solution: commit publicly to a cadence (two posts/week), which creates external accountability. He compares this to Alexandre Dumas writing *The Three Musketeers* as a serial for a magazine -- the deadline produced a masterpiece.

4. **The newsletter-as-book strategy** -- Some newsletter posts are secretly chapters of a forthcoming book (*The Software Engineer's Guidebook*). The newsletter's recurring deadline forces consistent output; the book emerges organically from the best pieces.

5. **Substack recommendations as growth engine** -- Growth accelerated dramatically after Substack introduced cross-newsletter recommendations in April 2022. The newsletter went from 50K subscribers (first 9 months) to 189K+ (next 5-7 months), gaining nearly 1,000 subscribers per day as a top publication.

6. **The downsides are real** -- Loneliness (no team, no water cooler). Guilt about unstructured time. No exit path (the business is tied to you personally). Mandatory output indefinitely (subscribers expect weekly value). Difficulty taking vacations. Stress from external validation metrics.

7. **Pedigree and depth matter** -- Working at respected companies (Skype, Uber) builds credibility that readers trust. But more importantly, real experience doing real things at scale provides the substance to write about. "If you don't have experience, don't start one" -- become an expert first, then share.

8. **Productivity hacks for solo creators** -- 20-minute forced focus timers, Python scripts that block Twitter/LinkedIn via the hosts file, the Centered app for accountability, and structuring the week so every day has a deadline for something.

## Concepts that deserve their own wiki page

- **[[Creator economy for technologists]]** -- The economics and lifestyle of full-time technical newsletter/content creation as an alternative to corporate engineering careers.
- **[[Deadline-driven productivity]]** -- Using public commitments and recurring deadlines to overcome the discipline gap of solo work.
- **[[Newsletter as business]]** -- Treating a paid newsletter as a one-person business with subscription economics, churn, and growth metrics rather than as a "creator" hobby.

## Notable quote

> "In my best year at Uber, I made about $330,000 in total compensation. Now I am making more than I made at Uber. And the difference is that my earnings keep going up as long as the newsletter is growing, so there's no theoretical cap on this."
