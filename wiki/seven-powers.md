# 7 Powers

The 7 Powers framework, developed by [[source-hamilton-helmer|Hamilton Helmer]] in his book of the same name, identifies seven — and only seven — sources of sustainable competitive advantage (what Helmer calls "power"). Each power must provide both a **benefit** (lower cost or higher price than competitors) and a **barrier** (something durable that prevents imitation). Warren Buffett's "castle protected by a moat" captures this — but Helmer insists you must verify it's a castle, not a shack.

## The seven powers

1. **Scale Economies** — Unit costs decline with volume (e.g., Netflix amortizing content costs across 200M+ subscribers)
2. **Network Economies** — Value increases with participation, but only when the advantage is *material* enough to tilt margins (rare; distinguished from mere network effects)
3. **Counter-Positioning** — New entrant adopts a model incumbents can't copy without damaging themselves (see [[counter-positioning]])
4. **Switching Costs** — Customers face meaningful costs to switch away
5. **Branding** — Durable psychological association that commands premium pricing
6. **Process Power** — Execution so complex and embedded that it becomes opaque and inimitable (e.g., Toyota, TSMC)
7. **Cornered Resource** — Exclusive access to a valuable asset (e.g., patents, talent, regulatory licenses)

## Key insights from the B3 episode

### Four powers for startups

Helmer narrows the actionable set for early-stage companies to four: **counter-positioning** (your starting refuge), then **scale economies**, **switching costs**, and **network economies**. Branding, process power, and cornered resource can be set aside initially — they either take decades to build or are circumstantial.

### Network effects ≠ network economies

Helmer's most provocative claim: many companies have network effects but few have **network economies**. [[uber|Uber]] and Lyft have network effects, but the advantage isn't large enough to materially tilt margins — Uber won through geographically-specific scale economies and attrition, not network power. The "flywheel" pitch is almost always overstated.

### Operational excellence is not power

Echoing Michael Porter, Helmer argues that execution excellence is essential but almost always imitable. It's a treadmill you must run on to survive, but it's not a barrier. Process power (execution so complex it becomes opaque) exists but is exceptionally rare.

### AI as electricity, not a new power

Helmer does not see an eighth power emerging from AI. He sees AI's biggest impact in the "tertiary" category — existing businesses made better by the technology, analogous to how electricity transformed factory floors. AI could, however, create powerful instances of existing powers (scale economies, switching costs, network economies) for specific businesses.
