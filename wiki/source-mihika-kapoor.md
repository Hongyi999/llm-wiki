# Source: Vision, Conviction & Hype — Building 0→1 Inside a Company ft. Mihika Kapoor

**Guest**: Mihika Kapoor (Product, Figma)
**Published**: 2024-04-21
**Duration**: ~1h 41m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uDq6_CPaRjM

## Executive summary

Mihika Kapoor, a design-engineer-PM hybrid at Figma and the company's go-to person for zero-to-one product development, breaks down the skills that make internal entrepreneurship work: crafting a vision rooted in user empathy, building high conviction quickly (even when imperfect), and engineering hype cycles to keep a fledgling product alive inside a larger organization. She frames the zero-to-one PM as the "keeper of the flame" — responsible for stoking embers when they risk dying out and spreading wildfire when the moment is right.

## Key ideas

1. **Vision is everything — and it's cross-functional from day one** — A compelling vision cannot emerge from a vacuum. It requires being "fundamentally inseparable" from both users and your team. At Figma, vision artifacts combine research insights, design prototypes, and engineering feasibility into a single shared deliverable — not a linear handoff from research to design to engineering. The pitch structure is: pain point → solution → proof point, repeated. Figma's culture is "see to believe" — words alone are insufficient; prototypes and mocks are built even before a project gets a green light.

2. **High conviction as a catalyst, not a conclusion** — Mihika advocates putting out an opinion (even if wrong) because people react more productively to a concrete idea than to nothing. She explicitly labels her confidence level: "I feel medium confidence on this, so I defer to you" vs. "I feel really strongly about this." The key balance: strong opinions weakly held — be willing to "kill your darlings" when user signal contradicts your conviction. Advice from Yuhki (CPO): "Go into research with at least an A- idea. If you learn something, get to A+. If not, at least you're not at a B."

3. **Hype is the fuel for zero-to-one survival** — A bottom-up project requires more hype energy than a top-down initiative to survive inside a company. Mihika inserted an unplanned demo of her barely-built new product into Figma's Sales Kickoff keynote — a "moment" that generated company-wide excitement and also produced valuable product insights. "You can't really create hype for something you don't believe in. The only way to create hype is to get people to see what you see." External hype matters too: FigJam's first birthday was celebrated as an Easter egg hunt of hidden features wrapped in birthday presents inside the product itself.

4. **The keeper of the flame metaphor** — Drawing from Hestia in Greek mythology (goddess who keeps the hearth burning while other gods go on quests), the zero-to-one PM must stoke flames when they risk dying out and fan them into wildfire when the moment is right. Three requirements for success: (1) have the right idea (empathy), (2) secure buy-in (vision), (3) make it spread (hype).

5. **Hackathons as zero-to-one launchpads** — Figma's "Maker Week" (company-wide hackathon) has produced some of its most important launches: the widgets platform, Jambot (AI plugin), and Multi-edit all started as hackathon projects. Mihika used Maker Week to pitch her new product by literally walking around the New York office asking people "Will you work on this with me?" She was simultaneously hosting the hackathon and building her own prototype — including a clever two-line code change that swapped the FigJam icon to a new icon in the file browser to make the product feel real.

6. **Culture as trust infrastructure** — Culture establishes trust that makes teams durable through hard times. Mihika started two Figma traditions: "Hot Seat" (a rapid-fire personal Q&A game at PM offsites that spread company-wide) and "The Figgies" (a scrappy, Amazon-sourced Oscar-style ceremony with absurd categories like "most likely to name their child Figma"). These low-cost rituals create emotional connection. Key insight from Vishal Shah (former Instagram head of product): "Culture is set top down; the innovation that comes out of that is bottoms up."

7. **Optimism that borders on delusion** — Mihika's first few pitches of the new product failed. It took pitching at a PM offsite (tepid response), then building it at a hackathon (breakthrough) before the idea gained company-wide traction. "The key to being successful at zero-to-one is to honestly have optimism that borders on delusion. You need to have an insane, almost reality distortion field where you don't hear the word no, or at the very least, you translate it into a not yet."

8. **Dogfooding as investment engine** — Figma has a multi-month staging/dogfooding process. Beyond getting product feedback, early internal release makes people across the company feel like they shaped the product — creating a virtuous cycle where co-creators become evangelists.

## B10 angle

Mihika's framework maps neatly onto [[mike-maples-jr|Mike Maples Jr.]]'s pattern-breaking actions: her "hype" is his "movements," her "vision pitching" is his "storytelling," and her persistence despite rejection echoes his "disagreeableness." The key difference is context: Maples describes these as startup founder skills, while Mihika demonstrates they work equally well for intrapreneurs inside large companies — validating Maples' final chapter on applying pattern-breaking principles at established companies. Her emphasis on hackathons as zero-to-one launchpads also connects to [[jessica-livingston|Jessica Livingston]]'s point about betting on scrappy hustlers — Mihika is essentially the hustler-founder archetype operating within Figma's walls.
