# Source: Todd Jackson — A Framework for Finding Product-Market Fit

Lenny's Podcast interview with [[todd-jackson|Todd Jackson]], partner at [[first-round-capital|First Round Capital]]. Published 2024-04-11. Todd shares the framework First Round uses in its PMF Method program (pmf.firstround.com), distilled from studying hundreds of their portfolio companies.

## The core framework

PMF is not binary. It has **four levels** and **three dimensions**, and is measured against a **persona/problem/promise/product** (the "[[four-ps-framework|Four Ps]]").

### The four levels of PMF

| Level | Name | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nascent | Rumors of interest, mostly founder pushing rocks uphill |
| 2 | Developing | A few users love it; usage is fragile |
| 3 | Strong | Pull from the market; retention and referrals both working |
| 4 | Extreme | Unambiguous, well-known PMF — "blowing up" |

See [[four-levels-of-pmf]] for the benchmarks on each dimension at each level.

### The three dimensions

Any claim of PMF should be triangulated across:
1. **Satisfaction** — e.g., Sean Ellis score, NPS, qualitative wow statements.
2. **Demand** — growth rate, organic pull, referral rate, pipeline velocity.
3. **Efficiency** — unit economics, CAC payback, burn multiple. Without efficiency, satisfaction and demand don't convert to a business.

Todd is emphatic: **60–70% of startups get stuck at L1 or L2**, usually because they conflate one strong dimension with PMF. A product can have satisfied users but terrible efficiency (subsidy-driven), or strong demand but zero retention.

### The Four Ps

A clear PMF story requires explicit answers to all four:
- **Persona** — exactly who is this for?
- **Problem** — what burning pain are they in?
- **Promise** — what specific outcome will you deliver?
- **Product** — how does the product embody the promise?

If any P is fuzzy, the founder is pre-PMF.

## The "$100 vending machine" metaphor

Credited to Brett Berson: imagine a vending machine that takes $100 and reliably outputs something worth $200. If you have one, your job is to feed it and scale it. Pre-PMF means you don't have the machine yet — and throwing money at an un-built machine just burns cash. The metaphor is a test of [[dollar-driven-discovery|dollar-driven discovery]]: is every dollar of spend producing a measurable, repeatable return?

## The marginal customer

A powerful lens: **look at your most recent 10 customers, not your first 10**. Your first customers are selected on founder relationship and enthusiasm. Your marginal customers reveal whether the motion is working on strangers. If the marginal customer looks nothing like the ideal one, you're selling on charisma not fit.

## Case studies (abridged)

- **[[vanta|Vanta]] (Christina Cacioppo)**: started with SOC 2 in spreadsheets, obsessively chasing a known pain. Fast to PMF because she started from the market.
- **Lattice (Jack Altman)**: pivoted from OKR tool (weak pull) to performance management (strong pull). Jack's heuristic: don't pivot on 10% signal; wait for 200% signal.
- **Plaid (Zach Perret)**: pivoted from a consumer PFM to the bank-API infrastructure powering everyone else. The second company was hiding inside the first.
- **Ironclad (Jason Boehmig)**: started as an AI legal assistant, pivoted to contract lifecycle management after seeing where customers actually paid.
- **Looker (Lloyd Tabb)**: used forward-deploy engineers to manually install the product with early customers, turning services work into signal.
- **Verkada (Filip Kaliszan)**: attacked a sleepy category (security cameras) with an opinionated product.
- **Persona (Rick Song)** — the "friend zone test": if every customer call ends with "this is cool, keep me posted," you're in the friend zone, not PMF.

## Heuristics Todd hammers

- **Dollar-driven customer discovery**: never trust words, only dollars committed. See [[dollar-driven-discovery]].
- **Wow statements**: listen for spontaneous superlatives ("I would cry if you took this away").
- **Madhavan Ramanujam's three-price question** for validating willingness to pay: what price feels fair, expensive-but-worth-it, and prohibitive?
- **Happy ears trap**: founders hear what they want. Counteract with written notes and dollar commitments.
- **The PMF timeline**: even successful companies take **4–6 years** to reach Extreme PMF. Impatience kills more startups than lack of talent.
