# Lessons from Airtable's unconventional growth strategy | Zoelle Egner

**Guest**: Zoelle Egner  
**Published**: 2023-01-29  
**Duration**: 1:13:52  
**YouTube**: [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0P8LMyeYl1U](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0P8LMyeYl1U)

## Executive summary

Zoelle Egner, employee #11 at Airtable, shares the unconventional marketing and growth tactics that helped a 15-person startup win six-figure enterprise contracts and build a legendary brand. She reveals how Airtable used psychographic targeting on Facebook ads, strategic billboard placement for enterprise credibility, lavish champion swag, and a manual Slack-integrated signup triage system to build champions at scale. The episode also covers her experience founding VaccinateCA, a volunteer-driven nonprofit that became the most comprehensive vaccine location database in the US. Throughout, Egner argues that customer success and marketing are fundamentally the same discipline and that every customer touchpoint either builds or destroys brand trust.

## Key ideas

1. **Punching above your weight through brand trust**: Small startups can appear much larger by obsessing over polish in every customer touchpoint — landing pages, emails, sample content, even swag. These signals tell customers 'we built this with you in mind' and build the trust needed for six-figure deals from a 15-person company.

2. **Champions over buyers**: Airtable's growth was driven by identifying and over-investing in champions — tinkerers who would evangelize the product internally — rather than targeting buyers directly. They built a Slack integration to manually triage signups and personally onboarded high-potential users, which bootstrapped both their persona models and their customer success motion.

3. **Billboards as enterprise credibility signals**: Airtable's billboards weren't about brand awareness — they were strategically placed near target enterprise offices so that when IT received a six-figure Airtable request, they'd think 'I've seen that company, they must be legitimate.' Remnant billboard inventory made this surprisingly affordable (low thousands per billboard).

4. **Customer success before sales**: Airtable invested in customer success before building a sales team, which is unconventional but created massive dividends. By making champions successful first, the sales conversation became 'you have 500 people using this product, it's time to pay,' making enterprise deals much easier to close.

5. **Elevate professions, not categories**: Instead of spending resources on category creation (e.g., getting a Gartner square), companies should elevate the professions their users belong to. A job is an identity people will fight for; a software category is a budget line item. Gainsight creating 'customer success' as a profession is the gold standard example.

## Concepts covered

- **Psychographic Targeting** — Identifying users by personality type and mindset (e.g., 'tinkerers') rather than demographics or job titles, then finding proxy interest clusters for ad targeting.
- **Champions at Scale** — A growth strategy focused on identifying, over-investing in, and empowering product champions who evangelize internally within companies, driving bottom-up adoption.
- **Inside-Company Virality** — The growth dynamic where one user within a company can spread a product to hundreds of colleagues by solving their workflow problems, more powerful than industry virality.
- **Profession Elevation** — A marketing strategy of elevating the professional identity of your users rather than creating a new software category, making your product part of their career identity.
- **Laughably Small MVP** — Starting with the absolute minimum viable product — even phones and spreadsheets for a life-saving service — to generate learning and impact before building sophisticated systems.
- **Customer Success as Marketing** — The thesis that customer success and marketing are fundamentally the same discipline — both identify needs, match customers to solutions, remove friction, and encourage sharing.
