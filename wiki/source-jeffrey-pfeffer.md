# Source: The paths to power — growing influence and advancing your career ft. Jeffrey Pfeffer

**Guest**: Jeffrey Pfeffer (Professor of Organizational Behavior, Stanford GSB)
**Published**: 2024-06-13
**Duration**: ~1h 23m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=etVCBDRXUH8

## Executive summary

Jeffrey Pfeffer teaches Stanford's most popular MBA course, "The Paths to Power," which he describes as "the cod liver oil of the Graduate School of Business — you know it's good for you, but you feel nervous about it." He lays out seven evidence-based rules for acquiring power and influence, arguing that political skill is not personality but a learnable set of skills — and that if good people don't acquire power, only bad people will wield it.

## Key ideas

1. **Get out of your own way** — The biggest obstacle to power is your own discomfort with it. Imposter syndrome, pre-emptory apologies ("I don't know if this is useful, but..."), and viewing power as dirty all prevent you from doing what is necessary. If you don't believe you deserve your position, that attitude leaks out and others stop believing it too.

2. **Break the rules** — Rules favor incumbents. Standing out requires doing something unexpected, just as disruptors in business do. Keith Ferrazzi at Deloitte ignored spreadsheets (which he was bad at) and instead created the Lincoln Quality Award, eventually becoming their first CMO. Tristan Walker signed up partnerships at Foursquare before being hired. Ask for what you want — the worst outcome of asking is the same as not asking.

3. **Appear powerful** — Research shows we respond mostly to how people look, secondarily to how they sound, and least to what they say. Eye contact, no notes, open body posture, louder voice, more gestures, and gentle touching all signal power. These are learnable skills: Steve Jobs could not give a presentation before Regis McKenna coached him. Jack Valenti (MPAA, 5'2") "felt taller than he was" through presence alone.

4. **Build a powerful brand** — No one promotes you if they don't know who you are. Substance without visibility means nobody knows your substance. Laura Chau made partner at Canaan in four years by strategically building visibility through writing, podcasting, and playing up her distinctiveness. Write a personal brand statement and get feedback.

5. **Network relentlessly** — The first principle of networking is generosity: "What can I do for you?" The more people you know, the more you can connect others, and brokers who connect people gain disproportionate power. Pursue weak ties (Granovetter's research) because strong ties provide redundant information. Omid Kordestani stopped doing his job at Netscape to network — his name appeared on every list when Google needed their first business hire. He became employee #11 and made $2.5 billion.

6. **Use your power** — Power is self-fulfilling: using it generates more resources, more success, and more people wanting to associate with you. Not using power when you have it means failing to do the job you were given. "If you want to be liked, get a dog."

7. **Success excuses (almost) everything** — People believe power is homeostatic (fly too close to the sun and you'll fall), but it's actually self-reinforcing. Bill Gates, Martha Stewart, and countless others had their transgressions forgiven or forgotten once they achieved success. This isn't how the world should work — it's how it is, was, and will be.

- **[[Networking as Generosity]]** — Reframing networking from self-serving to connecting people who benefit from knowing each other
- **[[Power vs. Autonomy Trade-off]]** — Pfeffer's insight: you can have power or autonomy, but rarely both

## B11 angle

Pfeffer's class is explicitly paired with [[carole-robin]]'s "Touchy Feely" as the two most popular Stanford GSB courses — one teaches the external game of influence, the other the internal game of relationships. His "get out of your own way" rule directly complements [[graham-weaver]]'s work on limiting beliefs and autopilot mode. The networking-as-generosity framework echoes [[phyl-terry]]'s emphasis on asking for help as a giving activity, and the "Doing Power" homework philosophy mirrors Weaver's insistence on accountability and action over theory.
