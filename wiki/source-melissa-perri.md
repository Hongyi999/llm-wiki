# Everything you've ever wanted to know about SAFe and the product owner role | Melissa Perri

**Guest**: Melissa Perri  
**Published**: 2024-11-10  
**Duration**: 1:24:20  
**YouTube**: [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wbi9chsAHp4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wbi9chsAHp4)

## Executive summary

Melissa Perri, author of Escaping the Build Trap and CEO of Product Institute, traces the history of the product owner role from its origins in the 2001 Agile Manifesto through Scrum's codification and the wave of enterprise agile transformations that created thousands of product owners without teaching them product management. She explains that the product owner role emerged from developers wanting someone to prioritize their backlog — not from product management — and that the two-day certification classes (CSPO) that mass-produced product owners taught backlog management but not customer research, experimentation, or strategy. Perri shares practical advice for organizations navigating this gap, including how Capital One successfully layered product management onto its agile transformation, and how Athenahealth retrained 365 product owners — with many voluntarily opting out when they understood the full scope of real product management.

## Key ideas

1. **Product owner is not product manager**: The product owner role was created by Scrum developers to have someone prioritize the backlog and write user stories. It was never designed to encompass market research, experimentation, data analysis, or strategy. The early Scrum Guide mentioned that a product manager 'may be' the product owner, but this note disappeared in the widely adopted 2013 version.

2. **Agile transformations created POs without PM skills**: Large enterprises (banks, insurers, telecoms) adopted Scrum to build software faster, ran two-day CSPO certification workshops, and turned business analysts, project managers, and developers into 'product owners' overnight. These people were trained to manage backlogs but not to discover what to build or measure outcomes.

3. **SAFe rarely works as designed**: The Scaled Agile Framework was created to scale Scrum across large organizations but in Perri's experience, every company that found success with SAFe ended up ripping it apart and making it into something else. It is often sold by consulting firms (including McKinsey) as a silver bullet for digital transformation.

4. **Lowercase-a agile is the goal**: Rather than dogmatic adherence to Scrum ceremonies, teams should focus on the core agile principle of releasing quickly and getting customer feedback. Small startups often do not need formal frameworks at all. When existing processes stop serving the team, they should be changed — inspect and adapt.

5. **The transformation playbook — baseline, train, sort**: At Athenahealth, Perri trained all 365 product owners, gave them opportunity to practice real PM skills, and let people self-select out. Many moved to operations, data, or UX research roles that better fit their strengths. Bringing in experienced directors to scatter across teams accelerated the leveling-up of remaining PMs.

## Concepts covered

- **[[product-operating-model|Product owner role]]** — A Scrum-defined role responsible for prioritizing the backlog, distinct from the full-scope product manager role that includes discovery, strategy, and experimentation.
- **SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework)** — A framework for scaling Scrum across large organizations that Perri finds rarely succeeds without significant customization.
- **[[product-operating-model|The Build Trap]]** — The pattern where organizations measure success by output (features shipped) rather than outcomes (customer and business value delivered).
- **Agile Manifesto** — The 2001 declaration by software developers establishing principles for iterative, feedback-driven software development.
- **CSPO certification** — A two-day Certified Scrum Product Owner workshop that teaches backlog management but not full product management skills.
- **Inspect and adapt** — Core agile principle of continuously evaluating whether current processes serve the team and changing them when they do not.
