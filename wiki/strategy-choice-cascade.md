# Strategy Choice Cascade

The Strategy Choice Cascade is Roger Martin's framework for making strategy decisions, introduced in his book *Playing to Win* (co-authored with A.G. Lafley). It structures strategy as five cascading choices, each constraining the next. In B3, [[source-roger-martin|Martin]] presents the framework alongside [[source-richard-rumelt|Richard Rumelt]]'s complementary approach (Good Strategy / Bad Strategy), and [[source-chandra-janakiraman|Chandra Janakiraman]] applies a strategy playbook influenced by both.

## The five choices

1. **Winning aspiration** — What does winning look like? (Not "participating" or "competing" — *winning*.)
2. **Where to play** — Which markets, customers, channels, geographies, and product categories?
3. **How to win** — What is the distinctive approach that will create sustainable advantage in your chosen arena?
4. **Capabilities** — What capabilities must be in place to execute the how-to-win?
5. **Management systems** — What systems, structures, and measures reinforce the choices above?

## Key insights from B3

### Playing to win vs. playing to play

Martin's central provocation is that most companies are "playing to play" — they have aspirations and activity but no actual strategy for winning. A real strategy requires making **choices that competitors find uncomfortable to mirror** (see [[counter-positioning]]). The test: if your strategy doesn't make you uncomfortable about what you're *not* doing, it isn't a real strategy.

### Differentiation vs. low cost

Martin identifies only two viable how-to-win strategies: **differentiation** (commanding a premium) or **low cost** (delivering acceptable value at the lowest cost). Everything else is stuck in the middle. He argues most technology companies should pursue differentiation because software margins naturally reward premium positioning.

### Betterment over perfection

Rather than seeking the theoretically optimal strategy, Martin advocates **betterment** — making your current strategy slightly better through iterative testing of your most uncertain assumptions. This mirrors the experimental approach of [[product-market-fit|PMF]] testing.

## Rumelt's complementary framework

[[source-richard-rumelt|Richard Rumelt]]'s *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy* provides a complementary lens:

- **Strategy kernel** = diagnosis + guiding policy + coherent actions
- **The crux** = the single most important challenge that, if solved, unlocks everything else
- **Bad strategy** = goals without a plan, fluff language, failure to identify the crux

Where Martin focuses on choices (where/how to win), Rumelt focuses on **diagnosis** (what is the crux?) and **coherent action** (what is the concentrated effort?). Together they cover both the "what to decide" and "how to think" dimensions of strategy.

## Relationship to adjacent concepts

- **[[seven-powers]]**: Helmer's framework answers "what structural power enables winning?"; Martin's answers "where to play and how to win." Complementary lenses on the same question.
- **[[counter-positioning]]**: The mechanism by which a how-to-win choice becomes durable — competitors can't copy without self-harm.
- **[[product-market-fit]]**: Strategy sets the arena; PMF confirms you've won in it. Martin's betterment-over-perfection mirrors iterative PMF testing.
