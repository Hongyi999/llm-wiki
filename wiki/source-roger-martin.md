# 5 essential questions to craft a winning strategy | Roger Martin (author, advisor, speaker)

**Guest**: Roger Martin  
**Published**: 2024-07-25  
**Duration**: 1:22:03  
**YouTube**: [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y7SN4FK8noY](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y7SN4FK8noY)

## Executive summary

Roger Martin, named the world's #1 management thinker by Thinkers50 and author of Playing to Win, delivers a masterclass on strategy for practitioners at every level. He defines strategy as 'an integrated set of choices that compels desired customer action' and walks through his Strategy Choice Cascade: five questions (winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, required capabilities, and enabling management systems) that must be answered as a reinforcing whole. Martin argues strategy is hard because it's integrative, emotionally intimidating, and poorly taught in business schools obsessed with the resource-based view of the firm. Using examples from Four Seasons, Southwest Airlines, Vanguard, Lego, Procter & Gamble's Olay, and Westlaw, he demonstrates that the strongest competitive advantages come from capabilities so multifaceted that competitors say 'life's too short' to replicate them. His parting advice: stop waiting—practice strategy through betterment, tackling the most painful gap first.

## Key ideas

1. **Strategy Choice Cascade—five reinforcing questions**: What is your winning aspiration? Where will you play (customers, channels, geographies, value chain position)? How will you win (differentiation or low cost)? What capabilities must you have? What management systems maintain those capabilities? These must be answered as an integrated, mutually reinforcing set.

2. **Play to win, not play to play**: If customers can flip a coin between you and a competitor, or a competitor can undercut your price while still profiting, you're not playing to win. You must be either meaningfully differentiated (Lego, Four Seasons) or the lowest-cost provider (Vanguard, Southwest)—there is no viable middle ground.

3. **Capabilities as unbreachable moats**: The strongest competitive advantages come from capabilities so complex and multifaceted that competitors say 'life's too short' to replicate. Westlaw's 100+ years of lawyer-written head notes, Four Seasons' 10% turnover rate vs. the industry's 80%, Southwest's entire business model requiring competitors to dismantle their hub-and-spoke systems—these are not single-factor advantages but reinforcing systems.

4. **Counter-positioning through 'won't' not just 'can't'**: Many competitive advantages persist not because rivals can't copy you, but because doing so would destroy their existing business. Walmart wouldn't build Amazon's website because it threatened stores; Estée Lauder wouldn't bring Clinique to mass channels because it would enrage prestige retailers. The best strategy makes competitors choose to leave you alone.

5. **Betterment over perfection—start with the most painful gap**: Don't try to solve all strategy at once. Identify the single most painful gap between current outcomes and desired outcomes, then ask which of the five cascade questions you could change to close it. Do this iteratively—like a professor who drops the worst-rated lecture each year—and over time you'll become a great strategist through practice.

## Entities discussed

- **Roger Martin** — Professor emeritus at Rotman School of Management, former dean, world's #1 management thinker (Thinkers50 2017), author of Playing to Win.
- **A.G. Lafley** — Former CEO of Procter & Gamble and co-author of Playing to Win with Roger Martin.
- **Michael Porter** — Harvard professor who created foundational strategy concepts in Competitive Strategy (1980), often caricatured by the resource-based view school.
- **Hamilton Helmer** — Author of 7 Powers, categorized by Martin as a practical non-academic strategist whose work fits into the 'how to win' box.
- **Bruce Henderson** — Founder of Boston Consulting Group in 1963, described by Martin as the father of practical commercial strategy.
- **Procter & Gamble** — Consumer goods company where strategy permeates to brand manager level, producing 10% of S&P 500 CEOs.
- **Four Seasons** — Luxury hotel chain whose strategy of 'home away from home' service, 10% staff turnover, and management-only model exemplifies multifaceted capability moats.
- **Southwest Airlines** — Low-cost airline that earned its cost of capital for 50 years through a counter-positioned business model competitors find 'life's too short' to replicate.

## Concepts covered

- **[[strategy-choice-cascade|Strategy Choice Cascade]]** — A five-question framework (aspiration, where to play, how to win, capabilities, management systems) for developing an integrated, reinforcing strategy.
- **[[strategy-choice-cascade|Playing to Win vs. Playing to Play]]** — The distinction between having a strategy that compels customer preference (win) versus merely participating without differentiation or cost advantage (play).
- **[[strategy-choice-cascade|Differentiation vs. Low Cost]]** — The two fundamental ways to win: either be meaningfully better in customers' eyes or have a structurally lower cost position.
- **[[counter-positioning|Counter-Positioning]]** — Positioning your strategy so competitors won't copy you because doing so would damage their existing business—the 'won't' is as powerful as 'can't.'
- **[[strategy-choice-cascade|Betterment over Perfection]]** — A practical approach to strategy: identify your most painful gap, make different choices to close it, then move to the next gap—iterative improvement over grand plans.
- **[[strategy-choice-cascade|Resource-Based View of the Firm]]** — A dominant academic strategy theory that Martin critiques as unhelpful because it focuses on building resources without guiding where to deploy them.
