# Source: Mental models for building products people love ft. Stewart Butterfield

**Guest**: Stewart Butterfield (co-founder of Slack and Flickr; sold Slack to Salesforce)
**Published**: 2025-11-20
**Duration**: ~1h 31m
**YouTube**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kLe-zy5r0Mk

## Executive summary

Stewart's mental models for building products people love: utility curves (value comes from crossing a threshold, not adding features), comprehension over friction (reducing thinking required matters more than reducing clicks), and taste as competitive advantage. Introduces "hyperrealistic work-like activities" (HRWLA) — meetings reviewing decks about reviewing decks — as a Parkinson's Law corollary. "We Don't Sell Saddles Here" philosophy: you're not selling the product, you're selling the outcome it unlocks.

## Key ideas

1. **Utility curves over binary features** — Products are designed as binaries (feature on/off), but value follows S-curves with thresholds. You can cut features but never compromise on quality; if something is 99% done, it's closer to 0%.
2. **Comprehension beats friction** — The obsession with reducing clicks is misplaced. The real challenge is comprehension — users need to understand what something does and why. Make decisions trivially easy by reducing thinking, not steps.
3. **Taste as competitive advantage** — Most companies don't invest in taste and craft. Being obsessive about details (the "tilt your umbrella" philosophy) creates emotional connections that drive viral adoption.
4. **Positioning creates the market** — "We Don't Sell Saddles Here" means selling outcomes, not products. Be a banana in a world of apples — fundamentally different so people can't be neutral.
5. **Hyperrealistic work-like activities (HRWLA)** — Work expands to fill time; organizations accumulate people doing meetings about meetings. Leaders must ensure known, valuable work exists and actively resist busy-work.
6. **Divine discontent** — In 2014, Stewart called Slack "a giant piece of shit." Successful product leaders are perpetually unsatisfied with quality.
