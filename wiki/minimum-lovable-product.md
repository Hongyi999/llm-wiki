# Minimum Lovable Product

A Minimum Lovable Product (MLP) is the smallest version of a product that users genuinely love -- not merely tolerate. It is a deliberate evolution beyond the Minimum Viable Product (MVP), which optimizes for learning with the least effort. The MLP raises the quality bar: instead of shipping fifteen features at a mediocre level, you ship five features exceptionally well and scatter moments of delight throughout. In a landscape of abundant alternatives, viability is no longer sufficient to earn adoption; users must feel an emotional pull toward the product.

## MVP vs. MLP

| Dimension | MVP | MLP |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Validate a hypothesis with minimum effort | Earn genuine user love with minimum scope |
| **Quality bar** | "Does it work?" | "Do users feel delight?" |
| **Feature count** | As few as possible to test | As few as possible, but each one polished |
| **Risk** | Over-building before learning | Under-delivering on craft |
| **Signal** | "I would use this" | "I would be very disappointed without this" |

The MLP concept aligns closely with the [[product-market-fit]] measurement tradition -- particularly the [[sean-ellis-test]], where the benchmark is not "do users find it useful?" but "would they be very disappointed if it went away?"

## Anton Osika: the company named after the concept

[[source-anton-osika|Anton Osika]] encoded the MLP philosophy directly into his company's name. [[lovable]] -- an AI engineer that turns natural language descriptions into working applications -- hit $4M ARR in four weeks and $10M in two months with just 15 people. Osika's product hierarchy is explicit:

1. **Minimum lovable product** -- the launch threshold
2. **Lovable product** -- the iteration target
3. **Absolutely lovable product** -- the north star

This emphasis on craft and user delight drove organic word-of-mouth growth without significant marketing spend. Osika argues that in the AI era, the bottleneck has permanently shifted from engineering capacity to **product taste** -- the ability to understand what users need and deliver it with care. When AI can generate any feature, the differentiator is which features you choose to build and how thoughtfully you build them.

His complementary insight is the "scaling law for AI reliability": systematically identifying the specific places where AI gets stuck (login flows, database persistence, payment integration) and painstakingly fixing each one. This debugging-focused approach is what makes the difference between a product that merely works and one that users love.

## Jiaona Zhang: MLP as hard-won lesson

[[source-jiaona-zhang|Jiaona Zhang]] (JZ) arrives at the MLP concept from a different angle -- years of product leadership at Dropbox, Airbnb, WeWork, and Webflow. Her formulation is direct: "Minimal lovable products is the new MVP."

JZ's most instructive case study is the **Airbnb Plus failure**. The initiative attempted to offer a premium tier of verified, high-quality listings, but it was driven by solution-first thinking rather than validated user need. The unit economics never worked -- verifying and maintaining quality listings at scale was prohibitively expensive. JZ draws two lessons:

1. **Do fewer things, but do them exceptionally.** Rather than shipping a broad feature set at a mediocre level, concentrate resources on the five things that will make users love the product.
2. **Scatter "pixie dust" of delight.** Select specific touchpoints where craft and surprise create emotional resonance. These moments of delight compound into word-of-mouth and loyalty.

JZ also emphasizes the importance of **doubling down on core love** -- understanding why existing users already love your product and investing deeply in that strength, rather than chasing adjacent competitors or spreading into new verticals.

## When MLP matters most

The MLP framework is especially critical in two contexts:

1. **Consumer products.** Switching costs are low, alternatives are abundant, and first impressions are decisive. Users who encounter a merely viable product will leave; users who encounter a lovable one will tell their friends.
2. **AI-powered products.** AI makes it trivially easy to ship mediocre features fast. When every competitor can generate the same capabilities, the differentiator becomes taste, polish, and the emotional quality of the experience. As Osika puts it, the bottleneck is no longer "can we build it?" but "should we build it, and can we make it delightful?"

## Contrasting perspectives

Osika and Zhang broadly agree on the principle but emphasize different aspects:

- **Osika** focuses on the MLP as a **launch philosophy** -- naming your company after it, making it the explicit quality gate for every release, and using it to drive organic growth in a zero-marketing-spend model.
- **Zhang** focuses on the MLP as a **prioritization discipline** -- a tool for saying no to feature sprawl, killing initiatives with broken unit economics (Airbnb Plus), and concentrating effort on what already works.

Both reject the notion that shipping fast means shipping rough. Speed and craft are not in tension when you ruthlessly limit scope.
